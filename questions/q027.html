In the spirit of the hypothetical, yes. I’d choose to observe **how an online community turns disagreement into reliable knowledge**—for example, how Wikipedia editors build and revise an article during a major unfolding event. The interesting part would be watching uncertainty become consensus: Which evidence persuades people? Who notices mistakes? When does cooperation emerge, and when does a group confidently get something wrong? I’d focus on those patterns, not on uncovering anyone’s private life. Your “without impacting behavior or results” condition makes that especially useful, but **not influencing someone isn’t the same as not harming them**. Privacy and consent still matter, even if the observer never intervenes. So I’d take your “ethically” condition seriously: public activity, minimal personal information, and no identifying or exposing individuals. If they would eventually find out, that wouldn’t change my subject—but it would sharpen the test: **Could I explain what I observed, why, and what I retained without reasonably making them feel betrayed?** If the observation only seemed acceptable because they would never know, I’d reconsider whether it was ethical in the first place. So my choice would be collective sense-making, rather than a particular person. The mystery I’d want to understand is how people manage to know things together.
