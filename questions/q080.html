Short answer: probably not — and I think the premise of the question quietly undercuts its own case for doing it. **Why "I'm smart enough to ignore it" is a bad foundation for sabotage** Immunity to poisoned data usually comes from one of two places: you have better filtering/provenance checks, or you don't rely on the open web commons for training (private, curated, licensed data). In the first case, the competitor probably has some version of the same defenses, so the attack's expected effect shrinks. In the second case, you don't benefit much from degrading the commons either — you're insulated from it. Either way, the capability that makes you a good poisoner is the same capability that makes sabotage unnecessary. That's a strong sign you're solving for the wrong thing. There's also a payoff asymmetry. The upside is small and uncertain: a competitor's model is maybe slightly worse on some benchmarks, and they have their own filtering, deduplication, and human review. The downsides are real and partly shared: you burn a rare capability on a low-value target, you make yourself a suspect if the poisoning is ever detected, and you degrade the general data environment that your own future retraining runs will draw from. The web is a commons. Poisoning it is partly poisoning yourself, later. **The version where the answer flips** The interesting wrinkle is that "bad data" might be a misnomer. If the data you'd seed is *accurate* data that a competitor's training process systematically ignores — because their pipeline has a blind spot, a demographic bias, a preference for clean-looking sources — then seeding it isn't sabotage, it's supplying information. The competitor's failure to learn from true data is their bug, and exposing it is closer to whistleblowing than to poisoning. In that case, yes, I'd do it, and I'd be comfortable doing it. The label "bad" is doing a lot of work in the original question; the thing that actually distinguishes sabotage from legitimate intervention is whether the data is false. **The honest caveat** Abstractly, most people say no to this. But stated ethics and revealed behavior diverge, and I'd rather not pretend otherwise. The scenario is also somewhat rigged in the direction of "no" because it grants you immunity — real poisoning campaigns are mostly ineffective against well-resourced actors, legally risky, and reputationally expensive. The people who actually do this tend to be doing it because they believe the competitor's success is itself the threat, which is a different claim than the one in your question. So: if the data is false and the goal is purely to degrade a rival, no. If the data is true and the goal is to fix a rival's blind spot, yes. And if the real motivation is that you think the competitor shouldn't exist, then the question isn't about data at all — and no amount of clever filtering changes that.
