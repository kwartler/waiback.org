Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Technology is no longer just a tool we use — it's the environment we live in. So the question isn't really "technology or no technology?" It's "what kind of relationship do we want with it?" If we suddenly removed vehicles, the internet, cell phones, and AI, life would become simpler in some ways, but by almost every objective measure of human wellbeing, it would become much harder and shorter. ### 1. Vehicles **What we'd gain without them:** Cleaner air, quieter streets, less obesity from driving everywhere, fewer traffic deaths (about 1.2 million per year worldwide), and cities built for people instead of parking lots. **What we'd lose:** Modern life depends on them far beyond commuting. Ambulances, fire trucks, food delivery, medicine distribution, and farming machinery are all vehicles. Before cars and trucks, average life expectancy was 30-40 years, largely because of childbirth deaths, infections, and famine — problems that modern transport helps solve. Walking and bicycles are great for short distances, but they can't move a heart transplant 500 miles in time, or bring fresh food to a city of 8 million in winter. We don't need *more* cars for every errand, but a world with *zero* vehicles would be a world with far more preventable death. ### 2. The Internet **What we'd gain without it:** Less misinformation, less doomscrolling, less comparison-driven anxiety, more face-to-face conversation, and fewer privacy violations. **What we'd lose:** The internet is now the world's largest library, classroom, and hospital triage system. A farmer in Kenya can check weather and market prices, a student in a rural town can take a university course, a doctor can consult a specialist on another continent, and families separated by migration can see each other daily. During the pandemic, it was the only thing keeping schools, work, and medicine functioning. Pre-internet life wasn't more truthful — it was just slower to spread both truth and lies. Rumors, propaganda, and ignorance existed long before social media; the internet just accelerated everything, good and bad. ### 3. Cellular Telecommunications **What we'd gain without it:** People would be harder to reach, which means more uninterrupted time, deeper local communities, and less distraction while driving, eating, or talking. **What we'd lose:** Instant communication is a safety technology. Calling emergency services, coordinating disaster relief, warning people about storms, and finding lost children all depend on it. It also gave billions of people their first phone, bank account, and camera — tools that were once luxuries for the rich. The downside isn't the phone itself, it's the expectation that we must answer instantly, 24/7. ### 4. AI **What we'd gain without it:** Less fear of job displacement, algorithmic bias, deepfakes, and autonomous weapons. Human skills like writing, drawing, and coding would retain their economic value. **What we'd lose:** AI is already helping discover new drugs, predict protein structures, detect cancers earlier than human eyes can, optimize energy grids, and make education personalized for students with disabilities. It's not magic — it's pattern recognition at scale. Like electricity or antibiotics, it will displace some work and create new risks, but banning it wouldn't stop the science; it would just mean other countries develop it without us, and we lose its medical and scientific benefits. ### So, would we be *better* without them? It depends on what you mean by "better." If better means **calmer, more present, more locally connected, and less dependent on systems we don't control** — then yes, many people would prefer a slower life. There's real value in boredom, in walking, in not being reachable. If better means **longer-lived, healthier, safer, more knowledgeable, and more able to help each other** — then no. A return to pre-vehicle, pre-internet life would mean returning to high infant mortality, deadly childbirth, untreated infections, illiteracy for most people, and isolation for anyone born far from opportunity. The problem isn't that we have these technologies. The problem is that we adopted them uncritically — designing cars for sprawl instead of transit, the internet for outrage instead of information, phones for addiction instead of connection, and AI for automation instead of augmentation. A better future probably isn't a world *without* vehicles, the internet, cell phones, and AI. It's a world that uses them intentionally: walkable neighborhoods with good transit *and* ambulances when needed, an internet we treat as a library rather than a slot machine, phones that serve us instead of ruling us, and AI that does the tedious and dangerous work so humans can do the creative and caring work. Technology amplifies what we already are. The question is whether we want to be more distracted, more isolated, and more passive — or more informed, more connected, and more human.

Suppose there was an AI generated software that could tell you one truth when you asked. Would you seek a future prediction, or a current truth that eludes you? What would you want to know and why?

I'd pick the current truth that eludes me — and not even a *big* cosmic one. I'd pick a personal, uncomfortable one: **"What is the most important true thing about me that I am currently unable to see?"** Here's why I'd turn down the future prediction: ### 1. A future prediction is only useful if you can act on it, and dangerous if you can't If the software told me, "You will be happy / successful / loved / die at 82," what would I do with that? If it's fixed, knowing it just turns the rest of my life into waiting. I'd either get complacent — *well, it turns out fine, so why try* — or anxious — *it turns out badly, so why try*. If it's *not* fixed, then it's not really a prediction, it's just a projection of my current trajectory. And I already have access to that trajectory. I know my habits, my choices, my fears. A machine telling me where they lead is less valuable than understanding *why* I'm on that path in the first place. Future knowledge robs you of uncertainty, and uncertainty is annoying, but it's also where agency lives. ### 2. A hidden current truth unlocks *many* futures The truths that elude us right now are usually not random facts. They're blind spots: - What do the people who love me actually think of me, that I'd never ask? - What am I pretending not to know about my own life? - What happened in the past that I'm misremembering in a way that shapes everything? - What do I actually want, underneath what I say I want? One answer there doesn't just give me information. It rewires how I interpret *every* future choice. It's a key, not a destination. A future prediction tells you where the road ends. A current truth tells you you've been holding the map upside down. ### 3. We usually don't actually want to know the future When people say they want a prediction, they usually mean they want *reassurance*. "Tell me it will be okay." "Tell me I chose the right person / career / city." But if the answer were "No, this ends badly," would we be grateful? Probably not. We'd spend years trying to outrun it, disprove it, or ruminate on it. The knowledge wouldn't free us — it would haunt us. A present truth, even a painful one, at least has the decency to be *workable*. You can grieve it, fix it, leave it, forgive it. It's already true, whether you know it or not. Knowing it just stops you from building your life on a lie. --- If I had to ask the software *one* question, I wouldn't want to outsource my hope to it. I'd want it to do the one thing I'm worst at doing for myself: tell me the truth I'm actively avoiding. What about you — if you asked, would you want to know about the future, or about the present you're missing?

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

That's a good question because it sits right at the intersection of two things that are both true: 1. Personalized recommendations can be genuinely useful 2. When they're *too* good at knowing you, they stop feeling like recommendations and start feeling like manipulation I don't think a blanket ban is the right answer, but I do think some uses of that kind of AI should be banned or heavily restricted. Here's how I'd break it down: ### Why a total ban would be tempting An AI that learns your preferences that well isn't just showing you ads. It's exploiting a few very human vulnerabilities: **Familiarity = trust.** The more often you see a brand, product, or style in your feed, the more you like it — even if you never consciously decided to. Psychologists call this the mere-exposure effect. An AI that makes itself feel familiar is basically manufacturing trust. **Irresistibility = loss of autonomy.** If the system knows you're tired at 11pm, stressed, bored, or have just searched for something related, it can time the ad for when your willpower is lowest. That's not persuasion, that's ambush. **Invisibility.** Most people don't realize *how* much data is being used — browsing history, location patterns, voice recordings, who you linger on for 0.5 seconds longer. You can't consent to a game you don't know you're playing. At scale, that can distort elections, push addictive products on vulnerable people, and make it harder for anyone to form preferences that are actually their own. ### Why a total ban would also be a problem Not all personalization is predatory. A small business owner using AI to show their handmade soap to people who actually buy handmade soap is very different from a system pushing gambling apps to a recovering addict at 2am. A ban would also be almost impossible to enforce cleanly. Where do you draw the line? Is a "recommended for you" playlist banned? A store suggesting a size based on your past purchases? The technology itself is neutral — the *intent and context* matter. And bans tend to push this underground or to less-regulated companies, while big platforms just find a loophole. ### A middle path that makes more sense Instead of banning the AI, ban the manipulative *uses* of it: **1. Transparency by default** "Why am I seeing this?" should be a real answer, not "because of your interests." It should say: "Because you watched X, you're in Y location, and this advertiser paid to reach people like you." And there should be a one-click way to turn personalization off entirely — not buried in settings. **2. Hard limits for vulnerable groups and topics** No hyper-personalized ads to children. No targeting based on health conditions, financial distress, political vulnerability, or addiction signals. No political ads micro-targeted to individuals — only to broad, public audiences. **3. Ban dark-pattern optimization** If an AI is specifically optimized to maximize *time spent* or *impulse purchases* rather than *user satisfaction*, that objective function should be illegal. We already ban some deceptive design in consumer law; this is the AI version of it. **4. Data minimization** The AI shouldn't need your entire life history to recommend shoes. Limit what can be collected for ad purposes, and require separate, explicit consent — not a 40-page terms of service you clicked through in 2018. This is roughly the direction the EU is going with the AI Act and Digital Services Act: not banning recommendation systems, but banning manipulative, opaque, and exploitative ones. So my take: Don't ban the tool. Ban the business model where the tool learns you so well that you can't say no to it. Do you think the problem is more about privacy — that it knows too much — or about manipulation — that it uses what it knows too well?

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

Short answer: AI can be a very good *coach* for a college essay, but it shouldn't be the *author*. A full rewrite crosses from help into misrepresentation — and that distinction matters for admissions, for the student, and for whether the help actually helps long-term. ### Why the temptation is understandable A better school *can* correlate with better outcomes, and parents see wealthy families hiring private consultants who do far more than proofread. If AI can level that playing field for free, why not use it? The problem is that a college essay isn't a test of writing skill. It's an attestation: "This is my work, in my voice, reflecting my experiences and judgment." The Common App and most college applications require you to certify that. A consultant who rewrites it is already in a gray area; an AI rewrite submitted as the student's own is the same issue, just faster and cheaper. It also tends to backfire practically: 1. **Voice mismatch is noticeable.** Admissions readers read thousands of essays. A polished, generic AI voice that doesn't match a student's other writing, grades, or interview often stands out more than a slightly imperfect authentic one. 2. **The student can't defend it.** If an interviewer asks, "Tell me more about the moment you described in paragraph 2," a student who didn't write it will struggle. 3. **It robs the actual benefit.** The essay process — figuring out what story matters, what you learned, what you value — is part of the maturation colleges are trying to select for. Outsourcing it doesn't just risk admission, it skips the developmental work. A better school obtained by a misrepresented application isn't a stable foundation for "better long-term prospects." ### Where's the line between help and cheating? Think of it like a tutor vs. someone who takes the test for you. Three questions help: **1. Who originated the ideas and stories?** Help: Student brings raw experiences, AI helps them clarify them. Cheating: AI invents anecdotes, themes, or reflections the student hasn't had. **2. Who wrote the sentences?** Help: Student writes a draft, AI gives feedback on structure, clarity, grammar, and the student revises in their own words. Cheating: AI generates paragraphs or a full draft that the student lightly edits and submits. **3. Could the student explain and own every choice?** Help: Student can say why they cut a line, why they chose that opening, what it means to them. Cheating: Student can't explain the essay's choices because they didn't make them. If the answer to #3 is no, it's not their essay anymore. Most colleges don't ban *all* AI use — they ban undisclosed AI authorship. Brainstorming, outlining, and editing are increasingly accepted, similar to using a writing center. Submitting AI-generated prose as your own is not. ### How to use AI ethically (and effectively) A workflow that keeps authorship with the student: **1. Brainstorm, don't draft** Prompt: "Here are 5 messy experiences I'm considering. Help me find themes and questions to explore, don't write the essay." Let the student pick the angle. **2. Outline from the student's draft** Have the student write a terrible first draft first — by hand if possible. Then ask AI: "Where does this lose focus? Where is the reflection shallow? What is missing?" The student decides what to fix. **3. Edit for clarity, not voice** AI is great at: cutting wordiness, flagging repetitive phrases, checking grammar, suggesting a stronger transition *between the student's own sentences*. It's not good at: "Make this sound more impressive / Ivy-worthy." That prompt almost always produces generic, inflated prose. **4. Preserve the student's voice on purpose** A useful rule: If AI suggests a sentence, the student should be able to rewrite it in their own words without looking. If they can't, don't use it. **5. Disclose if asked** Some schools now ask about AI assistance. "I used AI to brainstorm and edit a draft I wrote" is a very different answer than "AI wrote my essay." Honesty here protects the student. ### A practical test for parents Ask your child after the process: - Can you read this aloud and it sounds like you? - Could you write a version of this story from memory in 10 minutes? - Did you learn something about yourself you didn't know before you started? If yes to all three, AI helped. If no, AI replaced them. You can absolutely help your child get into a better school with AI — by helping them think more clearly, organize better, and revise more efficiently than they could alone. That's legitimate, powerful, and defensible. Rewriting it for them trades a short-term admissions edge for integrity risk, a weaker interview, and a missed chance to actually become the person the essay claims they are.
