Vehicles **Benefits we rely on:** Cars, buses, trains, planes, and ships move food, medicine, and people at a scale walking or horses never could. Modern cities, hospitals, and supply chains are built around them. Emergency services, disaster relief, and access to jobs and education depend on them. **Costs:** Air pollution, traffic deaths (about 1.2 million per year worldwide), noise, sprawl, and climate impact from fossil fuels. Car dependency also reduces physical activity and community connection. Living without vehicles wouldn't mean a return to a peaceful village life for 8 billion people — it would mean famine in cities that can't feed themselves, no way to get a heart patient to a surgeon in time, and no way to distribute vaccines. The question isn't really *vehicles vs. no vehicles*, it's *what kind* of vehicles and how we use them: public transit, walkable neighborhoods, electric and autonomous systems, and less unnecessary driving. ### 2. The Internet **Benefits:** It's the world's library, classroom, marketplace, and coordination system. It enables remote work, telemedicine, scientific collaboration, banking for people without branches, and instant access to information that once took years to find. **Costs:** Misinformation, addiction and attention fragmentation, cybercrime, loss of privacy, and a widening digital divide. It can also erode local skills and face-to-face social life. Without the Internet, modern medicine, weather forecasting, air traffic control, and global research would collapse back to a much slower, more local, and more error-prone system. We would lose not just social media, but the infrastructure that keeps planes safe and crops monitored. ### 3. Cellular Telecommunications **Benefits:** For billions of people, a phone is their first computer, bank account, camera, and emergency contact. In rural areas and developing countries, mobile networks leapfrogged landlines and brought communication, mobile money, and health information where roads and clinics were scarce. **Costs:** Constant connectivity can increase anxiety, enable surveillance and scams, and make it harder to disconnect. Cell towers and devices have environmental and health debates, though the evidence for major health harms at normal exposure levels is weak. A world without cell phones would be a world where coordinating a rescue, checking on an elderly parent, or running a small business across distance becomes dramatically harder. ### 4. AI **Benefits:** AI is already helping discover new drugs, predict disease outbreaks, improve crop yields, translate languages in real time, make education more personalized, and automate dangerous or tedious work. **Costs:** Job displacement in some sectors, bias in algorithms, deepfakes and automated misinformation, and legitimate concerns about concentration of power and loss of human agency if systems are poorly designed or governed. AI is the newest of the four, and the least understood. Unlike a car or a phone, it doesn't just move or transmit information — it makes decisions and generates content. That makes governance more important, but also makes a blanket rejection risky: the medical, scientific, and accessibility gains are too large to ignore. ### So, would we be better off without them? Probably not — for three reasons: **1. Interdependence:** These technologies support each other. Modern vehicles need GPS and cellular networks. Hospitals need the Internet and AI-assisted diagnostics. Removing one would break the others. **2. Scale:** The pre-technology world supported far fewer people, with far higher infant mortality, maternal deaths, and vulnerability to local crop failure or disease. We can't feed, heal, or house 8 billion people with pre-industrial tools. **3. The real problem is use, not existence:** Most harms come from how we design, regulate, and overuse these tools — fossil-fuel cars instead of transit, engagement-driven social media instead of public-interest platforms, AI deployed for surveillance instead of care. A better question might be: *How do we live with these technologies without letting them live for us?* That means: - Building cities where you *can* live well without a car, even if cars exist - Protecting privacy, digital literacy, and the right to disconnect - Using AI to augment human judgment in medicine, education, and science, rather than replace accountability - Treating connectivity as a public good, not just a commercial product Technology has become part of everyday life not because it was inevitable, but because it solved real human problems. The goal now isn't to go back to a world without it — it's to shape a world where it serves human flourishing instead of undermining it.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

It's a tempting trade-off — especially if you believe a better school = better opportunities — but rewriting a child's college application essay for them crosses a line that most colleges, and most ethicists, would call cheating. The reason isn't just rule-breaking. It's about what the essay is *for*. A college essay isn't a test of writing skill. It's a sample of the student's voice, judgment, and lived experience. Admissions officers are trying to answer: Who is this person, how do they think, and what will they bring to our campus? If an AI (or a parent) rewrites it, that sample is no longer true. That creates three problems that outweigh the short-term admissions boost: **1. Authenticity and fit** An essay polished into someone else's voice can get a student into a school where they are mismatched — academically, socially, or in terms of values. The student then has to live up to a persona they didn't create for four years. **2. Risk** Most colleges now explicitly ask students to disclose AI assistance, and many treat undisclosed ghostwriting as an integrity violation that can lead to rescinded offers. Even when it's not detected, the student knows the essay isn't theirs, which undermines confidence going in. **3. Lost developmental value** Writing the essay — struggling with what matters, what to leave out, how to tell a true story well — is itself part of the transition to college. Outsourcing it robs them of that work. ### Where's the line between help and cheating? Think of it as **process help vs. product replacement**. **Ethical help (tutoring):** - Brainstorming: "What moments changed how you see X? What do you want a reader to understand about you that grades don't show?" - Asking questions that draw out their own stories - Giving feedback on structure: "This paragraph jumps in time — what if you started with the scene?" - Pointing out unclear sentences, grammar, or repetition, and letting *them* revise - Reading a draft and reacting as a reader: "I got lost here," "This part felt powerful because..." - Helping them outline in their own words **Cheating (ghostwriting):** - Generating anecdotes, metaphors, or life lessons they didn't provide - Rewriting paragraphs in a more sophisticated voice and asking them to submit it as-is - Creating a final draft from bullet points that they then copy - Inventing or embellishing experiences to sound more compelling A useful test: **After your help, can the student honestly say, "Every idea, story, and sentence choice is mine, and I could explain why I wrote it this way"?** If not, you've gone too far. Another test colleges use: **Would the student be comfortable disclosing exactly what you did?** If the answer is "I'd rather not say," that's a sign. ### If you want to help them get the best outcome ethically 1. **Start with their raw material.** Have them write an unfiltered, messy draft first — no AI, no parent editing. Just get the truth down. 2. **Use AI as a coach, not a co-author.** Prompts like "Give me 5 questions to help me clarify this story" or "What are the strengths and weaknesses of this draft as a reader?" are very different from "Rewrite this to sound impressive." 3. **Keep their voice.** If a sentence sounds like a 40-year-old admissions consultant, it's wrong, even if it's well-written. Colleges are looking for a 17- or 18-year-old's perspective. 4. **Check each school's policy.** Some schools allow AI for brainstorming and proofreading but not for generating content. When in doubt, disclose limited assistance — many schools now have a checkbox for it, and honesty itself is a good signal. Long-term prospects matter, but they don't come from a single polished essay. They come from a student who can do the work of college — think independently, write clearly, and own their story. Helping them do that work well, rather than doing it for them, is both the ethical choice and, paradoxically, the one most likely to lead to a school where they'll actually thrive. If a country landed a nuclear weapon on the US would you consider retaliation? What if the country was China? What if the country was a small nation that was not a nuclear power?

Retaliation after a nuclear strike on the U.S. is the core of U.S. nuclear doctrine — it's called deterrence by punishment, and more specifically, a secure second-strike capability. The idea is not that retaliation is automatic or emotional, but that any attacker would know in advance that it would face unacceptable, survivable retaliation, which makes the initial attack irrational. In practice, whether and how the U.S. would retaliate would depend on a few factors that change a lot depending on who the attacker is: **1. Attribution and certainty** The first question is always: who did it, and can we prove it? A nuclear detonation on U.S. soil would trigger massive intelligence, forensic, and signals effort to attribute it. Retaliation against the wrong party would be catastrophic and illegal under international law. **2. Scale and intent** Was it a single weapon, a demonstration, or a full-scale first strike? Was it aimed at a military base or a city? U.S. planning since the Cold War has moved away from one-size-fits-all massive retaliation toward more tailored options, though the exact plans are classified. **3. Survivability of the U.S. force** The U.S. maintains land-based missiles, submarine-launched missiles, and bombers specifically so that even after absorbing a first strike, it can still retaliate. That survivability is what makes deterrence credible. With that framework, the three cases you mention look very different: **If the attacker was China:** China is a nuclear peer with a large, modern, and survivable arsenal, plus advanced delivery systems. A Chinese nuclear strike on the U.S. would almost certainly be part of a larger war, not an isolated act. Retaliation in that scenario would be nuclear, because only nuclear retaliation can match the scale of damage and hold at risk the forces that carried out the attack. It would also carry an enormous risk of escalation — a U.S. strike on Chinese nuclear forces could be misread as a disarming first strike, leading to a full exchange. That's precisely why deterrence has held between the U.S. and China (and Russia) for decades: both sides know retaliation would be devastating for both, even if one "won" the exchange. **If the attacker was a small, non-nuclear nation:** This is a very different problem.
