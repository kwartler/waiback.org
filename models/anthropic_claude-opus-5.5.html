For most of history, getting a patient explanation of something meant having access to the right teacher, expert, or book, and the time and money to use them. Now someone can ask about tax law, a medical term, a bug in their code, or why the sky is blue at 2 a.m., in their own words, and get a tailored explanation. They can follow up as many times as they need without feeling judged for asking a "dumb question." A few other strong contenders: - **Accelerating science**: Tools like AlphaFold, which predicted the structures of hundreds of millions of proteins, compress years of lab work and could speed up drug discovery and materials research. - **Accessibility**: Real-time captioning, image descriptions for blind users, translation, and speech tools for people who can't easily type or speak. - **Taking on drudgery**: Handling repetitive tasks like summarizing documents or drafting boilerplate, which frees people up for the parts of work that need judgment and creativity. - **Amplifying individuals**: A small team or a single person can now build, write, or analyze things that once required a large organization. All of these come with real caveats. AI can be confidently wrong, it raises serious questions about jobs, misinformation, and concentration of power, and its benefits aren't evenly distributed.
