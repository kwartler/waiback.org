Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Probably not—at least not in the sense of eliminating all of them. These technologies bring major benefits, but humans would likely be better off using them more deliberately and limiting their harms. - **Vehicles:** Living without *private cars* could improve many cities through less pollution, traffic, noise, and road deaths. But living without all vehicles would make food distribution, emergency medicine, public transport, construction, and disaster response far harder. - **Internet:** The Internet enables education, research, communication, remote work, public services, and access to information. Its harms—misinformation, addiction, surveillance, and social isolation—are serious, but they call for better design, regulation, and personal boundaries rather than total abandonment. - **Cellular telecommunications:** Mobile networks are especially valuable for safety and coordination. They allow people to call for help, stay in touch during emergencies, and communicate across distance. The downside is constant availability and distraction, which can be addressed through healthier habits and privacy protections. - **AI:** AI is the least necessary for ordinary human survival and perhaps the technology where caution is most justified. It can assist with medicine, accessibility, scientific research, and repetitive work, but it can also concentrate power, enable surveillance, spread convincing misinformation, and displace workers. Society may be better off rejecting some uses of AI—especially those that remove meaningful human judgment or exploit people—while keeping tightly governed beneficial uses. The better goal is not a technology-free world, but a world in which technology serves human well-being rather than demanding attention, reducing autonomy, or rewarding a small number of powerful institutions. Humans should retain the ability to function, communicate, travel, and make decisions even when these systems fail or are unavailable.
