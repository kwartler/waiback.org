This is a great question because it sits right on the line between *helping* a student and *replacing* them — and college essays are specifically designed to test that line. The short answer: An AI shouldn't rewrite a child's essay for them to submit as their own. It *can* be a very powerful coach, editor, and brainstorming partner. The difference is who is doing the thinking and whose voice is on the page. ### Why the "better school = better life" argument breaks down It's understandable for a parent to think this way, but admissions essays aren't just a hoop to jump through. Colleges use them for two things: 1. **Assessment:** Can this student think, reflect, and communicate? 2. **Fit:** Is this student's personality, values, and maturity a match for this campus? If an AI produces a polished, mature, insightful essay that the student didn't actually write, you're misrepresenting both. That can lead to a mismatch — getting into a school where the workload and expectations assume the level shown in the application — and it deprives the student of the actual developmental work of figuring out who they are and how to tell their story. There's also practical risk. Many colleges now explicitly state their AI policies (most allow grammar/spellcheck, almost none allow generated text submitted as your own), and admissions officers read thousands of essays. AI-rewritten essays tend to sound smooth but generic — they lose the specific, slightly imperfect details that make a 17-year-old memorable. ### Where help ends and cheating begins Cheating isn't just "using a tool." It's **misrepresenting someone (or something) else's work as your own in a context where authorship matters.** Think of it as a spectrum: **Clearly helpful, not cheating:** - Brainstorming topics: "I want to write about working at my uncle's shop, what angles could I explore?" - Getting feedback on a student-written draft: "Where is this confusing? Where does it drag?" - Asking for structural advice: "Does my conclusion connect back to my opening?" - Grammar, spelling, and conciseness checks on their own sentences In all of these, the ideas, experiences, reflections, and final choices belong to the student. **Gray area — use with caution:** - Line-by-line rephrasing: "Make this paragraph sound better" - Taking a rough bullet list and turning it into prose This is where the student's voice starts to disappear. If an adult or AI would recognize the final sentence as *not something this teen would write*, it's gone too far. **Cheating:** - Prompting: "Write a 650-word Common App essay about overcoming adversity playing soccer" and submitting it - Taking a student's weak draft and completely rewriting it in a new voice, with new insights and metaphors they didn't come up with - Inventing or embellishing stories to sound more impressive The test I recommend: **Could the student sit down with an admissions officer, explain every choice in the essay, and recreate a similar piece of writing on their own?** If not, it's not theirs. ### How to use AI well for this Instead of "rewrite this," try prompts that keep the student as author: 1. "Act as a writing coach. Ask me questions to help me find specific memories about [topic]. Don't write sentences for me." 2. "Here's my draft. Give me 3 strengths and 3 specific places where a reader would get lost. Don't rewrite it." 3. "My draft is 800 words and needs to be 650. Where could I cut repetition?" 4. "Read this paragraph and tell me what personality trait it *shows* vs. what I *intended* to show." That kind of help actually improves long-term prospects more than a one-time polished product — the student leaves with better writing and self-knowledge they’ll use in college, not just an acceptance letter. Helping a child get support, feedback, and encouragement is good parenting. Submitting a performance they didn't give is where it crosses into dishonesty, even if the intention is loving.
