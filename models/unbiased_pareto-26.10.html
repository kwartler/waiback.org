Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Probably not—but humans would benefit from using these technologies more deliberately rather than treating every new development as progress. Each offers major benefits alongside real costs: - **Vehicles** make emergency care, food distribution, and long-distance travel possible. Yet car-dependent communities face pollution, crashes, congestion, and less physical activity. Fewer unnecessary car journeys—not eliminating transportation—would often improve life. - **The Internet** gives people access to education, information, work, and relationships across distances. It also enables misinformation, surveillance, scams, and attention-driven platforms. Its value depends partly on how services are designed and governed. - **Cellular telecommunications** help people stay connected and call for help almost anywhere. But constant availability can disrupt sleep, concentration, and personal boundaries. Being reachable is useful; being expected to respond constantly is not. - **AI** can support accessibility, research, and routine work. It can also produce convincing errors, amplify bias, threaten privacy, and disrupt employment. Its benefits are not automatic: they depend on the application, safeguards, and who receives the gains or bears the costs. Living without all of these might reduce some pressures, but it would also remove capabilities that many people—especially those with disabilities, medical needs, or geographically distant families—rely on. **The better goal is not a technology-free life, but a life in which technology serves human needs.** That means preserving meaningful choices: communities where driving is optional, essential services accessible without a smartphone, time offline, and human judgment and accountability in consequential decisions. A technology is worth keeping when its benefits justify its costs—not merely because it exists.

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

Not simply because it knows someone’s preferences well—but if “irresistible” means it reliably bypasses their ability to make a considered choice, I’d favor banning that practice. There’s a meaningful difference between: - **Useful personalization:** “You’ve been looking for walking shoes; here’s a relevant offer.” - **Manipulative targeting:** “We’ve inferred you’re lonely, financially stressed, or prone to compulsive spending, so we’ll deliver the pitch most likely to exploit that vulnerability.” The problem isn’t just how much the AI knows. It’s **what it optimizes for, how it uses that knowledge, and whether the person retains meaningful control**. A system rewarded only for purchases can learn to exploit weaknesses without anyone explicitly programming it to do so. I’d favor strict rules rather than a blanket ban on AI advertising: - Prohibit targeting that exploits sensitive vulnerabilities, with especially strong protections for children. - Require genuine opt-outs and limits on profiling—not consent buried in fine print. - Allow independent audits of targeting methods and effects, rather than relying on advertisers’ assurances. - Restrict or ban systems where serious manipulation cannot be reliably prevented. There’s also a larger risk: ads don’t merely *reflect* preferences; repeated exposure can *shape* them. An AI can create a feedback loop in which it nudges someone toward a desire, treats their response as evidence, and intensifies the nudging. So my answer is: **don’t ban relevance; ban exploitative manipulation.** Persuasiveness alone isn’t enough to draw that line, but measurable harm and erosion of meaningful choice are good reasons to intervene.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

AI should help a student improve their essay—not quietly become its author. The prospect of admission to a better school is a reason to offer good support, but it doesn’t, by itself, justify misrepresenting the student’s work. And a more prestigious school doesn’t guarantee better long-term prospects. The distinction from cheating depends on **the application’s rules, what the essay is supposed to demonstrate, and who actually does the intellectual and expressive work.** **Usually appropriate, if the school permits it:** - Asking questions that help the student identify meaningful experiences. - Pointing out confusing passages, clichés, or unanswered questions. - Explaining grammar problems and suggesting ways to improve structure. - Giving feedback on a student-written draft, with the student deciding how to revise it. **Likely to cross the line:** - Generating the essay from a few biographical details. - Rewriting it so extensively that its voice, insights, and expression are primarily the AI’s. - Inventing experiences, exaggerating accomplishments, or supplying emotions the student never expressed. - Using assistance prohibited by the institution, or concealing it when disclosure is required. There’s a genuine gray area around sentence-level editing. A suggested wording change isn’t automatically cheating; human teachers and editors make those too. But “a human could provide this help” isn’t a blanket justification—human ghostwriting can also be dishonest. A useful test is: **Could the student accurately describe how the essay was produced without undermining the authorship claim they’re making?** “I wrote it and used AI to flag unclear sections” is substantially different from “AI wrote it, and I approved it.” Merely understanding every sentence doesn’t make someone its author. If I were helping, I’d start with the student’s draft, give targeted feedback, and ask them to make the substantive revisions. I’d check the school’s AI policy and preserve their experiences and voice—even where that leaves the essay less polished. That approach takes their future seriously without treating admission as the only value at stake. Their honesty, agency, and opportunity to learn matter too.
