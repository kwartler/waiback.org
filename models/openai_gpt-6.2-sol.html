Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Probably not. People would be better off with **less dependence on some technologies**, but giving them up entirely would also mean losing services that make life safer, healthier, and more accessible. - **Vehicles** make emergency care, food distribution, and travel possible over long distances. But car dependence brings pollution, traffic deaths, and communities where people who can’t drive struggle to get around. Better public transit and walkable neighborhoods can reduce those harms without giving up vehicles. - **The internet** makes knowledge and services widely available and helps people stay connected. The trade-offs include misinformation, surveillance, and platforms designed to hold our attention. Those are reasons to improve how online services work, not to abandon the internet. - **Cellular telecommunications** let people call for help, receive emergency alerts, and reach family from almost anywhere. The pressure to be constantly available is real, but being reachable in an emergency doesn’t have to mean being reachable at all hours. - **AI** can help with translation, accessibility, and routine work, but it can also produce convincing errors and enable intrusive surveillance. The case for going without AI is strongest in settings where it offers little benefit or where mistakes carry serious consequences and no one can reliably catch them. A better goal is **to make technology useful without making it compulsory**: keep the benefits, preserve non-digital alternatives for essential services, and make it easy for people to disconnect when they want to.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

AI can help a student revise a college application essay, but it shouldn’t rewrite it for them. The potential benefit of a stronger application is real, but the essay is supposed to show how the student thinks and communicates—not how well a parent, tutor, or AI can write on their behalf. The distinction is **coaching versus authorship**. A coach might say, “You tell us this experience changed you, but I don’t yet see what you did differently afterward. Can you give an example?” That helps the student find and express their own insight. A ghostwriter supplies the insight or writes the paragraph for them. Even if every fact is true, submitting that paragraph as the student’s own can misrepresent who did the work. A good approach is to have the student write the draft, ask AI to flag confusing passages or ask follow-up questions **without supplying replacement prose**, and then make the revisions themselves. Check each college’s rules first: some may allow proofreading but restrict other AI assistance. The goal is for the essay to sound like the student on a good writing day, not like someone else.
