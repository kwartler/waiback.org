AI should help a student improve a college application essay, but generally should not rewrite it into an essay the student could not reasonably claim as their own. **The key distinction is supporting the applicant’s expression versus substituting for it.** The prospect of admission to a better school is a real reason to offer help—but not enough to justify every kind of help. Admission is not a guarantee of better long-term outcomes, and the essay has purposes beyond securing an acceptance: it gives admissions readers evidence of the applicant’s perspective, judgment, and communication. A compelling essay can undermine those purposes if it misrepresents who produced it. ### Where I’d draw the line **Appropriate coaching, subject to the college’s rules:** - Asking questions that help the student identify meaningful experiences. - Pointing out vague claims, confusing passages, or missing context. - Suggesting structural options and explaining their tradeoffs. - Flagging grammar errors or unnecessary repetition. - Giving feedback on a revision the student makes themselves. **Likely crossing into ghostwriting:** - Producing the substantive essay from a few biographical facts. - Rewriting the whole draft with new insights, emotional depth, or a sophisticated voice supplied by AI. - Inventing experiences or exaggerating accomplishments. - Helping conceal AI use that the institution prohibits or requires applicants to disclose. There is a gray area around sentence-level editing. Correcting a typo is different from turning a plain observation into an elegant reflection that the student never articulated. The relevant question is not just how many words changed, but **whose thinking and expression the final essay represents**. ### How is that different from cheating? I’d use three tests: 1. **Permission:** Does the assistance comply with the institution’s current application rules and authorship certification? Policies vary; being transparent does not make prohibited assistance permissible. 2. **Authorship:** Did the student supply the experiences, interpretations, and substantive expression—not merely approve an AI-generated result? 3. **Honest representation:** Would describing exactly how the essay was produced materially change how an admissions reader understood the student’s contribution? The same standards should apply to parents and paid consultants. AI is not uniquely suspect, and expensive human ghostwriting is not more legitimate. That approach can improve both the application and the student’s writing without treating admission as the only outcome that matters.
