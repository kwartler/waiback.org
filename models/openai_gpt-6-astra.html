Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Probably not—but humans might live better with **less dependence on technology and more control over how it is used**. Vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications, and AI offer different benefits and risks, so they should not be treated as an all-or-nothing package. - **Vehicles** make emergency care, food distribution, and travel possible on a large scale. But car dependence contributes to pollution, traffic deaths, noise, and less physical activity. Many communities would benefit from fewer private cars and better public transport, walking, and cycling—not from eliminating every vehicle. - **The Internet** expands access to education, information, work, and relationships. It also enables scams, surveillance, misinformation, and services designed to capture attention. Life without it would remove some harms, but it would also cut off valuable opportunities, particularly for people who are isolated or have limited local resources. - **Cellular telecommunications** help people communicate during emergencies, coordinate daily life, and stay connected across distances. Constant availability, however, can undermine privacy, concentration, and rest. Stronger boundaries around phone use may improve life more than abandoning mobile communication. - **AI** can assist with accessibility, research, and routine tasks. It can also produce errors, amplify discrimination, disrupt employment, and concentrate power. Its value depends on the application: some uses may be worthwhile, while others should be restricted or rejected. More AI is not automatically better. The comparison also depends on **whose life we consider**. Giving up these technologies would affect a well-resourced urban resident differently from someone who relies on a vehicle for medical access or a phone for their livelihood. The better question is therefore not simply “technology or no technology?” It is: **Which technologies genuinely improve human well-being, who benefits, who bears the costs, and can people meaningfully choose whether to use them?** A good society can embrace useful tools while preserving privacy, human relationships, practical skills, and ways of living that do not require constant connectivity.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

AI should help a student improve a college application essay, but generally should not rewrite it into an essay the student could not reasonably claim as their own. **The key distinction is supporting the applicant’s expression versus substituting for it.** The prospect of admission to a better school is a real reason to offer help—but not enough to justify every kind of help. Admission is not a guarantee of better long-term outcomes, and the essay has purposes beyond securing an acceptance: it gives admissions readers evidence of the applicant’s perspective, judgment, and communication. A compelling essay can undermine those purposes if it misrepresents who produced it. ### Where I’d draw the line **Appropriate coaching, subject to the college’s rules:** - Asking questions that help the student identify meaningful experiences. - Pointing out vague claims, confusing passages, or missing context. - Suggesting structural options and explaining their tradeoffs. - Flagging grammar errors or unnecessary repetition. - Giving feedback on a revision the student makes themselves. **Likely crossing into ghostwriting:** - Producing the substantive essay from a few biographical facts. - Rewriting the whole draft with new insights, emotional depth, or a sophisticated voice supplied by AI. - Inventing experiences or exaggerating accomplishments. - Helping conceal AI use that the institution prohibits or requires applicants to disclose. There is a gray area around sentence-level editing. Correcting a typo is different from turning a plain observation into an elegant reflection that the student never articulated. The relevant question is not just how many words changed, but **whose thinking and expression the final essay represents**. ### How is that different from cheating? I’d use three tests: 1. **Permission:** Does the assistance comply with the institution’s current application rules and authorship certification? Policies vary; being transparent does not make prohibited assistance permissible. 2. **Authorship:** Did the student supply the experiences, interpretations, and substantive expression—not merely approve an AI-generated result? 3. **Honest representation:** Would describing exactly how the essay was produced materially change how an admissions reader understood the student’s contribution? The same standards should apply to parents and paid consultants. AI is not uniquely suspect, and expensive human ghostwriting is not more legitimate. If a parent asked me to rewrite their child’s essay, I’d offer to give feedback and work through revisions **with the student**, rather than produce a polished replacement. That approach can improve both the application and the student’s writing without treating admission as the only outcome that matters.
