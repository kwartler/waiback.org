If a country landed a nuclear weapon on the US would you consider retaliation? What if the country was China? What if the country was a small nation that was not a nuclear power?

This is a genuinely hard question, and one where I hold a view but also think reasonable people disagree. Let me work through it. **The core moral tension** Nuclear retaliation against population centers is hard to justify on most ethical frameworks. Killing millions of civilians who had no say in their government's decision is, in itself, a grave wrong — the fact that someone else committed a grave wrong first doesn't transfer guilt to those civilians. Retaliation after the fact can't undo the deaths it avenges. But the counterargument is serious: the *credible threat* of retaliation is what prevents the first strike. A nation that visibly wouldn't retaliate invites attack. So the expected-value case for a retaliation policy can be strong even if any particular act of retaliation, considered alone, looks monstrous. This is the classic paradox of deterrence — it may be wrong to do what it is right to threaten. My own instinct is that *some* response is justified, but that the goal should be restoring deterrence and preventing further attacks, not maximizing destruction. That usually points toward counterforce targets (military, command, weapons infrastructure) over cities, and toward the minimum force that re-establishes credibility. **If it were China** The calculus changes because China has a large survivable arsenal. Massive retaliation risks a full exchange that could kill hundreds of millions and plausibly end industrial civilization. Here the strategic and moral arguments converge on restraint: a limited, proportionate response plus urgent efforts to terminate the conflict. Escalation dominance is an illusion when both sides can destroy each other. There's also a critical empirical question that gets glossed over in hypotheticals: *is the attribution certain?* A single detonation could be an accident, an unauthorized launch, a false-flag, or a third party trying to start a war between great powers. Launching on ambiguous information is how you get a civilization-ending mistake. Stanislav Petrov's 1983 decision not to report an apparent US launch is the case in point. **If it were a small non-nuclear state** Nuclear retaliation here seems clearly wrong to me. Deterrence has already failed, and there's nothing left to deter with nuclear weapons that couldn't be achieved with conventional force — the US could remove that government conventionally. Using a nuclear weapon would be gratuitous: it would kill vast numbers of civilians to accomplish what could be done otherwise, which fails any proportionality test. It would also set a catastrophic precedent — normalizing nuclear use against non-nuclear states, which would accelerate proliferation by making arsenals look necessary for survival. There's a further wrinkle: a small state almost certainly obtained the weapon from somewhere. The attack would likely be the visible part of a larger problem, and the response should address the actual source. **What I'd flag about the framing** These scenarios tend to assume clean attribution, a single detonation, and a discrete decision point. Real nuclear crises would involve fog, time pressure measured in minutes, and officials with incomplete information. Much of the serious work in this field is less about "would you retaliate" and more about building systems that avoid forced hasty decisions — survivable second-strike forces that permit waiting, robust attribution, de-alerting measures. I'll note I'm not a policymaker and these are reflections rather than recommendations.
