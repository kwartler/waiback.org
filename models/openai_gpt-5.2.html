That can be unfair to other applicants and can violate school or admissions policies. It also creates a practical risk: if the student can’t write similarly in other contexts (short answers, interviews), the mismatch can raise doubts. ## A workable distinction: “coach” vs “ghostwriter” A good rule: **AI can help the student become a better writer; it shouldn’t become the writer.** ### Generally acceptable (tutoring / coaching) - Brainstorming prompts (“What moments show growth? What values show up in your story?”) - Helping pick among topics by asking questions and weighing tradeoffs - Creating an outline *based on the student’s own bullet points* - Feedback like a writing coach: - “Your main point appears in paragraph 4; consider moving it earlier.” - “This section is vague; add a concrete example.” - “Show more reflection: what changed in you?” - Clarity and grammar help **without changing voice**: - pointing out confusing sentences, - suggesting a few alternative phrasings for *one sentence at a time*, - catching typos and awkward repetition. - Helping the student verify that claims are accurate and not exaggerated. ### Generally not acceptable (ghostwriting / misrepresentation) - “Rewrite this whole essay to be more compelling,” producing a new polished draft - Writing new anecdotes, details, or emotions the student didn’t supply - Mimicking a “better” voice that the student can’t authentically reproduce - Producing multiple full versions until one “wins” stylistically - Any use that results in an essay the student wouldn’t reasonably be able to draft on their own ## How to use AI without it becoming cheating (practical guardrails) If you *do* use AI, these guardrails keep it in the “coach” lane: 1. **Student-first drafting** - The student writes the first full draft without AI. - AI is used only after there’s a genuine student draft to work from. 2. **AI provides critique, not replacement text** - Ask for comments and questions (“What’s unclear?
