Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Short answer: For almost every dimension of human well-being—health, safety, knowledge, opportunity, freedom, and even environmental stewardship—completely giving up vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI would make life worse, not better. What would be better is to use each technology more responsibly and design it so that its benefits are distributed widely and its harms are minimized. Below is a concise look at how life would change if we eliminated each technology, why most outcomes would be negative, and what alternatives or reforms are realistically preferable. ------------------------------------------------- 1. Vehicles (cars, trucks, trains, aircraft, ships) ------------------------------------------------- What we would lose • Rapid movement of food, medicine, and emergency aid • Trade networks that support billions of jobs • Daily mobility for education and work, especially outside dense cities • Organ transplants, disaster relief, fire services, ambulances Likely outcomes • Massive spikes in food prices and famines in regions that rely on imported staples • Re-localization of economies, leading to fewer choices, higher costs, and job losses • Increase in deaths from treatable medical emergencies and delayed care • Net increase in animal suffering: draft animals would return for freight and farming Environmental angle • Yes, fossil-fuel vehicles are big emitters, but eliminating modern transport would also eliminate supply chains for solar panels, wind turbines, vaccines, textbooks, and virtually all modern green tech. • The realistic fix: electrify transport, improve public transit, redesign cities—not abolish vehicles. ------------------------------------------------- 2. The Internet ------------------------------------------------- What we would lose • Instant global communication, research, telemedicine and distance learning • The infrastructure of modern finance, logistics, weather prediction and science • Access to free knowledge for billions (e.g., Wikipedia, open-source software, MOOCs) • Digital civic movements, whistle-blowing, and real-time disaster alerts Likely outcomes • Economic contraction: the Internet currently contributes ~15% of global GDP growth • Resurgence of gatekeeping by a small number of media outlets and publishers • Reduced innovation tempo; scientific collaboration would slow dramatically • Social isolation for diasporas; higher barriers for marginalized voices to be heard Problems that wouldn’t disappear • Misinformation, extremism and surveillance existed long before the Internet— they would simply migrate to print, radio or word-of-mouth, often with less transparency and fact-checking. Realistic fix • Stronger privacy laws, better digital literacy education, platform accountability—not turning off the network. ------------------------------------------------- 3. Cellular telecommunications ------------------------------------------------- What we would lose • Low-cost connectivity for 5+ billion people, many of whom never had landlines • Mobile banking that lifts millions out of extreme poverty (e.g., M-Pesa in Kenya) • Real-time coordination for health workers, farmers and supply chains • Immediate access to emergency services almost anywhere Likely outcomes • Sharp rise in disaster-related deaths (earthquake, tsunami alerts rely on SMS) • Economic setbacks in developing regions where mobile is the primary Internet entry point • Re-creation of digital divides along urban/rural and rich/poor fault lines Realistic fix • Tackle e-waste, improve cybersecurity, expand affordable coverage—not ban cell towers. ------------------------------------------------- 4. Artificial Intelligence ------------------------------------------------- What we would lose • Medical diagnostics that already outperform radiologists in some tasks • Early warning systems for climate modeling, earthquakes and pandemics • Efficiency gains in energy, agriculture, logistics, and drug discovery • Accessibility tools: real-time transcription, computer vision for the blind, language translation Likely outcomes • Slower scientific progress and higher R&D costs • Fewer options for aging societies facing labor shortages • Missed breakthroughs in sustainability (e.g., optimized battery chemistries, grid management) Risks that must be managed • Job displacement, biased algorithms, concentration of power, potential military misuse Realistic fix • Regulation, transparency, ethical guidelines, worker retraining— not abolishing the field. ------------------------------------------------- Common arguments for “living without modern tech” and the counters ------------------------------------------------- 1. “People were happier before.” Counter: Objective indicators—life expectancy, child mortality, literacy, extreme-poverty rates—have all improved dramatically alongside these technologies. 2. “Tech causes isolation.” Counter: It can, but it also connects distant families, enables niche communities, and supplies mental-health resources 24/7. The key variable is how we design platforms and manage our attention. 3. “Tech destroys the planet.” Counter: Every sustainability scenario consistent with 1.5 °C warming relies on advanced tech: renewables, smart grids, precision agriculture, AI-optimized materials, efficient transport. 4. “Low-tech lifestyles are simpler and more meaningful.” Counter: Personal choice matters; anyone can adopt low-tech living. Forcing it on society strips others of life-saving tools and opportunities. ------------------------------------------------- The reasonable path forward ------------------------------------------------- • Incentivize greener, more inclusive versions of each technology (e.g., EVs, open Internet, universal broadband, interpretable AI). • Strengthen governance: privacy laws, antitrust enforcement, ethics boards and global standards. • Equip citizens with digital, media and data-literacy skills from early education onward. • Provide robust safety nets and re-skilling programs to absorb labor market shocks. Conclusion A blanket rejection of vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI would roll back hard-won gains in health, knowledge and equality. The wiser goal is not subtraction but smarter, fairer and more sustainable addition—harnessing technology’s upside while aggressively mitigating its downsides.

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

Whether an ultra-personalised ad-recommendation AI should be banned depends on how it is built, deployed and governed. The same technology can be used to help people discover genuinely useful products—or to manipulate, exploit cognitive biases and erode autonomy. Instead of a blanket prohibition, most ethicists, policy-makers and technologists argue for a layered approach: 1. Define unacceptable practices • Micro-targeting that exploits legally protected attributes (race, religion, sexual orientation, political affiliation) or sensitive inferences (health, addictions, financial distress) should be illegal. • “Dark-pattern” optimisation—designs that systematically push users toward purchases or behaviours they would not choose if fully informed—should likewise be prohibited. • Behavioural targeting aimed at minors deserves outright bans or very narrow exceptions. 2. Make influence transparent • Every ad should be clearly labelled as such and accompanied by an easily accessible “Why am I seeing this?” explanation. • Users must be able to view and edit the profile data and preference signals the system has inferred. 3. Give users real control • Opt-in for highly granular targeting; the default should be broad contextual ads. • One-click global opt-out from personalised ads across an entire platform (and ideally across competing platforms via a standard like the Global Privacy Control signal). • Periodic “sanity checks” that remind users they are being profiled and ask whether they want to continue. 4. Impose design and audit requirements • Risk assessments before large-scale deployment (similar to the EU AI Act’s “high-risk” classification). • External audits of training data, recommendation objectives and live outcomes—for bias, manipulation and consumer harm. • Restrict optimisation targets: instead of pure click-through or time-on-site, include user-reported satisfaction or well-being metrics. 5. Align incentives • Platform revenue models based solely on engagement tend to breed manipulative targeting. Diverse revenue streams (subscriptions, federated ad exchanges, profit-sharing with users) reduce pressure to exploit vulnerabilities. • Fines for manipulative campaigns should exceed potential ad profits; repeat offenders could lose access to data-driven targeting altogether. 6. Special safeguards for sensitive domains • Political, employment, housing and credit ads require added transparency, public archival and severe penalties for discriminatory targeting. • Health-related ads should be limited to contextual placements vetted for accuracy. 7. Strengthen privacy law • Prohibit data brokerage that stitches together off-platform activity without explicit consent. • Enforce data-minimisation: only the signals strictly necessary for the stated ad objective may be processed. 8. Encourage technical alternatives • On-device or federated learning models that keep raw user data local and share only aggregate updates. • Differential privacy or secure multi-party computation to minimise leakage of personal information. Summary opinion A narrowly focused ban on exploitative practices—rather than on the entire class of personalised recommendation systems—protects user autonomy while preserving legitimate commercial and informational benefits. When these systems are transparent, consent-based, audited and limited in their inference scope, they need not be banned. When they cross ethical lines—manipulating users without meaningful consent or preying on vulnerabilities—they should indeed be disallowed and penalised.

A piece that sounds “professionally optimized” is usually obvious. • Reflection and self-knowledge – They want evidence that the applicant can think, feel, and grow, not a flawless op-ed. • Integrity – Every application includes an academic-honesty attestation. Permissible AI use—treat it like a smart tutor Allowed/wise: • Brainstorming prompts (“Generate questions I can ask myself about a service project I did.”) • Organizing ideas (“Here are my points; suggest a logical outline.”) • Identifying unclear spots or grammatical issues. • Vocabulary checks (“Is this word too obscure?”) • Practicing revision cycles (“Give me three ways to shorten this paragraph by 15 %.") Practical guardrails for parents, counselors, and students a. Start with the student’s own draft, in their own words. b. Use AI interactively—pose questions, get feedback, but keep the keyboard in the student’s hands. c. The best long-term life prospects come from cultivating authentic skills and integrity, the very qualities colleges want to see.
