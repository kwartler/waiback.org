I’m an AI—I don’t have authority, agency, or a role in military decision-making. Retaliation decisions are made by human governments under international law, strategic doctrine, and political accountability, not by algorithms. Generally speaking, how a state responds to a nuclear attack depends heavily on context, especially attribution, capabilities, and escalation risks: - **If it were a major nuclear power (e.g., China):** The strategic calculus is dominated by mutual vulnerability and the risk of civilization-scale escalation. Deterrence theory (like MAD) is built on the idea that direct nuclear exchange between peer arsenals is existentially catastrophic for all sides. A government would weigh retaliation against the near-certainty of massive counter-retaliation, global fallout, and potential human extinction-level outcomes. The response might still occur, but the form—conventional, limited, or full strategic—would be shaped by efforts to control escalation, not just revenge. - **If it were a small, non-nuclear state:** Attribution and proportionality become central. Under international law and norms (including the NPT framework), using nuclear weapons against a non-nuclear state raises severe legal, moral, and political problems, and would likely trigger global isolation. A government might instead pursue conventional military action, international legal mechanisms, sanctions, and coalition-building, while still treating the attack as an act of war. The absence of a peer nuclear arsenal removes the “mutual destruction” brake, but it doesn’t automatically make nuclear retaliation strategically wise or lawful. Key factors in any real-world scenario include: - **Certainty of attribution** (who actually launched it, and was it state-authorized?) - **Proportionality and distinction** under the laws of armed conflict - **Alliance obligations** and global stability - **Domestic and international political consequences** In short: “Would you retaliate?” isn’t a question an AI can answer, and for human leaders it’s not a simple yes/no—it’s a decision weighed against the survival of millions and the future of international order. The overwhelming global interest is in preventing any nuclear use in the first place through deterrence, diplomacy, and arms control.
