Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Probably not. Humans could live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications, or AI—people did for most of history—but it would not necessarily be “better” overall. Each technology has serious downsides: - **Vehicles** create pollution, traffic deaths, urban sprawl, and dependence on fossil fuels. - **The Internet** can spread misinformation, enable surveillance, addiction, scams, and social isolation. - **Cellular telecommunications** can make people constantly reachable, distracted, and less present. - **AI** can threaten jobs, privacy, fairness, and even safety if poorly controlled. But these technologies also bring major benefits: - **Vehicles** allow access to work, hospitals, food, education, and emergency services. - **The Internet** gives people knowledge, communication, commerce, education, and community. - **Cellular networks** are essential during disasters, medical emergencies, and daily coordination. - **AI** can improve medicine, science, accessibility, productivity, language translation, and decision support. A world without these technologies might be simpler and slower, and perhaps healthier in some social or environmental ways. So the better question may not be whether humans should live **without** these technologies, but whether we can live with them **more wisely**. That means: - designing technology around human well-being, not just profit; - reducing pollution and energy use; - protecting privacy and civil rights; - limiting addictive design; - ensuring AI is transparent, safe, and accountable; - teaching people when to disconnect. In short: humans would not necessarily be better off without modern technology, but we would be better off if we used it with more restraint, responsibility, and ethical control.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

AI can help with a child’s college application essay, but it generally should **not “rewrite” it in a way that substitutes the AI’s voice, judgment, or life story for the student’s own**. The essay is meant to show who the applicant is—how they think, what they value, how they express themselves. If AI turns a rough student essay into something the student could not plausibly have written, that crosses an ethical line and may violate admissions rules. A good distinction is: > **AI as coach or editor: usually acceptable. > AI as ghostwriter: not acceptable.** ## What AI can ethically help with AI can be used similarly to a teacher, parent, counselor, or writing tutor. For example, it can help the student: - Brainstorm possible essay topics - Identify which topic is most personal or revealing - Clarify the essay’s structure - Point out confusing sections - Suggest where more detail or reflection is needed - Check grammar, punctuation, and wordiness - Help reduce length to meet a word count - Ask questions that help the student deepen the essay - Explain why a sentence or paragraph is weak This kind of help preserves the student’s authorship. Example of acceptable AI help: > “Here is my essay draft. Please give feedback on clarity, structure, and whether my personality comes through. Do not rewrite it for me.” Or: > “Can you point out places where the essay feels generic and suggest questions I should answer to make it more personal?” ## What would likely be cheating It becomes ethically problematic when AI: - Writes the essay from scratch - Rewrites the essay so extensively that it no longer sounds like the student - Invents stories, accomplishments, emotions, or motivations - Makes the student appear more mature, eloquent, or experienced than they are - Produces polished language the student does not understand or could not defend - Creates a strategic “admissions-optimized” persona rather than an authentic self-portrait If the final essay is substantially the AI’s work, then the student is presenting someone else’s writing as their own. That is very close to plagiarism or misrepresentation. ## The key test: whose work is it? A useful test is: 1. **Did the student choose the topic?** 2. **Are the memories, examples, and insights genuinely theirs?** 3. **Did the student write the main draft?** 4. **Can the student explain every sentence and why it is there?** 5. **Does it sound like a polished version of the student, not a different person?** 6. **Would the student feel comfortable disclosing that AI gave feedback but did not write the essay?** If the answer to those is yes, the use is likely defensible. ## Why “better life prospects” does not fully justify it It is understandable to want to help a child get into the best possible school. Parents routinely pay for tutors, test prep, counselors, and essay coaching. But college admissions is partly a trust-based process. The essay is one of the few places where the student’s own voice matters. Using AI to manufacture a stronger essay may help in the short term, but it teaches the wrong lesson: that representation matters less than outcome. It can also backfire if the essay feels too polished, generic, or inconsistent with the rest of the application. The goal should not be to create the “best possible essay” in the abstract. It should be to help the student create the **best honest version of their own essay**. ## A practical ethical boundary A good rule: > AI may improve the student’s expression, but it should not replace the student’s thinking. Acceptable: - “This paragraph feels unclear—what are you trying to say?” - “You mention failure, but the lesson is vague. Can you add a specific moment?” - “Here are three possible ways to organize your existing ideas.” - “This sentence is grammatically awkward; here is a cleaner version.” Not acceptable: - “Write a compelling Common App essay about how my child overcame adversity.” - “Make this sound like a top Ivy League applicant.” - “Rewrite this entire essay in a more sophisticated voice.” - “Invent a stronger story from these facts.” ## Best approach If you want to use AI responsibly, have the child drive the process: 1. Student writes a first draft alone. 2. AI gives feedback, not a rewrite. 3. Student revises in their own words. 4. AI checks clarity, grammar, and word count. 5. Student makes final decisions. 6. Student reviews the college’s AI-use policy, if available. You can even prompt AI with a constraint like: > “Do not rewrite this essay. Give me feedback as a college essay coach. Focus on clarity, authenticity, structure, and places where I should add more personal reflection. Keep my voice intact.” ## Bottom line AI should not rewrite a child’s college essay in the sense of ghostwriting or transforming it into something the child did not truly author. But AI can ethically help as a tutor, editor, and source of feedback. The difference from cheating is **authorship and honesty**: if the ideas, experiences, reflection, and final wording are substantially the student’s, AI assistance is a tool. If the AI creates the substance or voice of the essay, it becomes misrepresentation.
