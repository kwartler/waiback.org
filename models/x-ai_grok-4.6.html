Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

**No, it would not be better for humans overall to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications, or AI.** These technologies have large downsides (pollution, distraction, inequality, new risks), but the historical record and measurable outcomes show they have raised living standards, health, knowledge, and coordination far more than they have harmed them. Abandoning them would mean reverting toward slower, more isolated, more labor-intensive, and more fragile conditions that most people already left behind. Vehicles (cars, trucks, planes, ships) enable food distribution, emergency response, trade, and access to jobs/healthcare that walking or animal power cannot match at scale. Without them, cities and supply chains as we know them collapse; famines, delayed medical care, and economic isolation become far more common. The costs—emissions, accidents, sedentary habits—are real and should be reduced with better engineering, policy, and alternatives, not by eliminating mobility. The Internet and cellular networks dramatically lowered the cost of information, communication, education, remote work, and coordination. Pre-internet/pre-mobile life meant slower news, limited research access, weaker global markets, and less ability to summon help or stay connected across distance. Downsides (misinformation, addiction, privacy erosion, attention fragmentation) are serious; they are better addressed by better tools, norms, and incentives than by going dark. Cellular specifically added location, real-time safety, and always-available contact that landlines never provided. AI (including earlier automation, search, recommendation, and current machine learning) is an amplifier of the above: it speeds diagnosis, logistics, scientific search, translation, and routine cognitive work. It also creates displacement, bias, and concentration-of-power problems. Completely forgoing it would slow progress on medicine, climate modeling, accessibility, and productivity without magically restoring “authentic” human life. Humans have used tools and cognitive offloading for millennia; the relevant question is governance and alignment, not abstinence. “Better” is partly subjective (some people thrive in low-tech settings and value certain kinds of slowness or locality). For humanity in aggregate, however, the data on life expectancy, literacy, caloric availability, infant mortality, and knowledge access point the other way. Romanticizing a pre-vehicle/pre-network/pre-AI world usually underweights disease, isolation, physical toil, and information scarcity. The productive path is to keep the gains, aggressively mitigate the harms (cleaner transport, better attention hygiene, privacy-respecting systems, safety research), and avoid both uncritical boosterism and Luddite nostalgia. Technology is not destiny; how we design, regulate, and use it is.

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

**No, it shouldn't be banned.** Highly effective, preference-aware ad recommendation is an intensification of something advertising has always done—match products to people more accurately than mass-market spray-and-pray. Outright prohibition would be a blunt, innovation-killing response that ignores trade-offs, existing consumer protections, and the fact that people already vote with their wallets and attention. ### Why a ban is the wrong tool - **Relevance is not inherently coercive.** Showing someone running shoes after they search for marathons, or a specific brand of coffee they repeatedly buy, is usually welfare-improving: less wasted attention, better product-market fit, and funding for free services. "Irresistible" is often just "this actually matches what I want." Humans have always been persuadable by skilled salespeople, packaging, and social proof; AI scales that without inventing a new category of harm. - **Bans don't stay narrow.** Once you ban "too familiar" personalization, regulators will expand the definition. We've already seen this with cookie rules, GDPR-style consent theater, and attempts to restrict targeting. The result is often worse UX, higher costs passed to consumers, and advantages for incumbents who can afford compliance. - **People retain agency.** Users can ignore ads, use ad blockers, opt out of personalization (where offered), switch platforms, or simply not buy. Impulse purchases and buyer's remorse predate AI. Treating adults as helpless against recommendations infantilizes them and underestimates revealed preference. ### Real risks that deserve scrutiny, not prohibition Hyper-personalization can exploit known psychological vulnerabilities (scarcity, social proof, variable rewards, FOMO) at scale, especially for people with weaker impulse control, addiction issues, or financial stress. It can also create privacy externalities if data collection is opaque or if inferences go beyond what users reasonably expect. Dark patterns that hide costs or make cancellation hard are already illegal in many places; they don't become legal just because an AI is doing them. The better responses are: - Transparency and meaningful consent (not 50-page policies). - Easy opt-outs from behavioral targeting. - Existing laws against deception, unfair practices, and targeting of protected classes or minors in harmful ways. - Competition so users have alternatives that compete on privacy or ad load. If an AI system is using stolen data, impersonating people, or running actual scams, that's already criminal—ban the *conduct*, not the recommendation engine. Effective advertising has always walked a line between informing and influencing. AI just makes the line more visible and the influence more precise. The solution is better rules of the road and user tools, not pretending we can un-invent targeting.
