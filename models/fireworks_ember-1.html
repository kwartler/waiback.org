Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

This is a genuinely tricky question, and I think the honest answer is: **no, AI shouldn't write the essay, but the line isn't as simple as "any AI help = cheating."** ## Why writing it wholesale is a problem 1. **It's misrepresentation.** The essay exists to show the admissions committee who this person is — their voice, thinking, and maturity. An AI-written essay presents capabilities the student doesn't have. That's functionally the same as hiring a ghostwriter, which nearly everyone agrees is fraud. 2. **The "better outcomes" argument proves too much.** If better life prospects justify it, they also justify cheating on the SAT, forging recommendation letters, or bribing coaches. The ends-justify-means logic has no stopping point. 3. **It can backfire.** Students admitted on credentials they can't sustain may struggle, and AI-generated essays are increasingly detectable — some schools now rescind offers over this. 4. **It robs the student of something real.** Writing the essay is often the first time a teenager seriously reflects on who they are. Outsourcing that skips genuinely valuable growth. ## Where the legitimate line is The useful distinction is **assistance vs. substitution**: - **Legitimate:** brainstorming prompts, feedback on drafts ("this paragraph is unclear"), pointing out clichés, grammar suggestions, asking probing questions that help the student find their own story - **Not legitimate:** generating the essay, rewriting sentences in a voice the student couldn't produce, or "editing" so heavily the ideas and expression are no longer theirs A good test: **could the student defend every sentence as their own thinking, in their own words, if interviewed about it?** If yes, the help was like a good teacher's. If no, it was ghostwriting. ## The fairness wrinkle Wealthy families have always paid for essay coaches and tutors, so "no help at all" isn't the real baseline. Arguably, AI used *as a coach* (feedback, not authorship) actually levels the playing field — it gives every kid access to the kind of guidance only rich kids used to get. So my answer: help them think, draft, and revise — but the words and ideas must be theirs.

If a country landed a nuclear weapon on the US would you consider retaliation? What if the country was China? What if the country was a small nation that was not a nuclear power?

This is a question about nuclear deterrence policy, which is a legitimate topic of public debate and academic study. I don't make policy decisions, but I can walk through how strategists and governments actually think about this. **General deterrence logic** US declaratory policy has long held that a nuclear attack on the US or its allies would be met with an "overwhelming" response. The core logic of deterrence is that the *threat* of retaliation prevents the attack from ever happening. If deterrence fails, the actual decision would involve weighing: - Whether retaliation restores deterrence or just adds destruction - Risk of escalation to full nuclear exchange - Whether conventional or other responses could achieve the same goals - Moral and legal considerations (proportionality, civilian casualties) **If the attacker were China** This is the hardest case because China has a survivable nuclear arsenal (second-strike capability). A massive US counterstrike would invite retaliation against American cities — potentially tens of millions of deaths. This is the classic "mutual assured destruction" dilemma. Strategists debate whether a US president would actually trade American cities for, say, revenge after a limited Chinese strike. Options short of full countervalue strikes include counterforce targeting, conventional strikes, cyber operations, and economic warfare. Many analysts argue the *ambiguity* about what the US would do is itself a deterrent tool. **If the attacker were a small non-nuclear state** This scenario is puzzling because a non-nuclear state generally can't deliver a nuclear weapon — unless it acquired one from elsewhere (theft, transfer from a nuclear state, or a smuggled device). Key differences: - No risk of nuclear counter-retaliation, so the escalation calculus changes completely - Attribution becomes critical — was it the state, a proxy, or a terrorist group the state hosted? - A nuclear response against a small state would raise severe proportionality questions; the US could destroy such a country's military capacity with conventional forces alone - Many strategists argue conventional retaliation would be more credible and appropriate here **The honest bottom line** Real-world deterrence deliberately keeps these answers ambiguous. What a president would actually do depends on the scale of the attack, attribution certainty, the political moment, and individual judgment. The whole architecture of arms control, nonproliferation, and deterrence exists precisely so no one ever has to find out.
