Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Short answer: No single blanket removal would be universally “better.” Each technology brings huge benefits and serious harms. A wiser goal is not total abstention but reshaping how we build, use, regulate and distribute vehicles, the Internet, cellular networks and AI so they serve human well-being, equity and ecological limits. Brief evaluation and trade-offs 1) Vehicles (cars, trucks, motorcycles) - Benefits: mobility, economic activity, emergency response, goods delivery, access to services and social life. - Harms: traffic deaths and injuries, air and noise pollution, greenhouse-gas emissions, land-use sprawl, sedentary lifestyles, inequality (dependence where transit is poor). - Feasible improvements: shift to public and active transport (walking, cycling), electrification, safer road design, urban planning that shortens travel needs, freight modal shift (rail), stronger safety and emissions standards. Eliminating vehicles outright would severely curtail economic activity and access in many places; reducing harmful impacts while preserving mobility is more realistic and desirable. 2) The Internet - Benefits: access to information, education, scientific collaboration, commerce, political organizing, cultural exchange. - Harms: misinformation, privacy erosion, surveillance capitalism, mental-health impacts for some, concentration of power, digital divides. - Feasible improvements: universal affordable access, media literacy, privacy protections and data portability, decentralization and open standards, regulation of monopolies and platform harms, stronger protections for journalists and vulnerable groups. Going offline wholesale would reduce many harms but also remove lifesaving access to knowledge, services and empowerment for millions. 3) Cellular telecommunications (mobile networks) - Benefits: instant communication, emergency response, financial services (mobile money), remote health, connection in low-infrastructure areas. - Harms: environmental cost of infrastructure, e-waste, surveillance and tracking risks, dependency on commercial networks. - Feasible improvements: durable, repairable devices; better e-waste systems and recycling; privacy-by-design, community networks and public options; resilient emergency networks. Removing cellular networks would especially hurt people in remote or under-resourced regions who rely on mobile connectivity for basic services. 4) Artificial intelligence - Benefits: improved diagnostics and drug discovery, personalized education, productivity gains, automation of dull/dangerous tasks, better resource management. - Harms: job disruption, biased or opaque decision-making, surveillance, misinformation (deepfakes), concentration of capability, potential misuse or safety risks at large scale. - Feasible improvements: careful governance (transparency, auditability, liability), human-centered design, safety testing, worker transition policies, public-interest AI, strong limits on misuse. Banning AI entirely would forfeit major medical, scientific and productivity gains; uncontrolled AI risks grave harms. The pragmatic route is strict rules and public oversight. Principles for deciding when to restrict, redesign or keep a technology - Proportionality: weigh benefits vs harms in context (healthcare AI vs manipulative recommendation systems). - Equity: ensure benefits reach marginalized groups and harms don’t fall disproportionately on them. - Accountability and transparency: systems that affect lives must be explainable, auditable and contestable. - Resilience and sustainability: design to minimize environmental footprint, promote repairability and redundancy. - Subsidiarity and human control: keep humans in meaningful control of high-stakes decisions and provide easy ways to opt out. - Democratic governance: involve citizens, not just companies or technocrats, in setting rules. Practical middle-path proposals - Invest in high-quality public transit, walkable cities and freight rail to reduce car dependency. - Guarantee affordable, universal Internet and mobile access while enforcing privacy, interoperability and anti-monopoly rules. - Implement robust AI governance: standards for safety, bias testing, transparency logs, licensing for high-risk systems, and funds for worker retraining. - Promote device repairability, right-to-repair laws and e-waste recycling to cut resource and environmental impacts. - Expand digital and media literacy so people can use technologies more safely and effectively. - Support community-controlled alternatives: municipal networks, cooperatives, open-source AI and local manufacturing. Conclusion Rather than choosing between utopia and Luddism, the better path is to keep the useful parts of these technologies while actively redesigning incentives, rules and systems so they reduce harms and serve human flourishing and planetary limits. In short: don’t throw away the tools—change how they’re made, who controls them, and how they’re used.
