Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

**No, it would not be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications, or AI as a blanket rule.** These technologies are not neutral add-ons; they are force multipliers for human capability. Pre-industrial travel was slow, expensive, and dangerous. Modern logistics (trucks, ships, planes, cars) underpin the global supply chains that reduced famine, made diverse nutrition routine, and supported specialization. Urbanization and economic growth since the 19th–20th centuries are inseparable from mechanized transport. The environmental and accident costs are serious, but the solution space is better vehicles and infrastructure (electrification, autonomy, public transit, urban planning), not abolition. - **Internet and cellular telecommunications**: These collapsed distance in communication and information access. Real-time coordination across continents, telemedicine, scientific collaboration, price discovery, education at scale, and the preservation/dissemination of knowledge all exploded. Billions gained access to libraries, markets, and social networks that previously didn't exist for them. The pathologies—addiction, misinformation, surveillance, atrophy of local ties—are real and well-documented. Yet the baseline alternative (information limited to what your village, class, or regime permits) was narrower, slower to correct errors, and often more parochial. The internet is the closest thing humanity has built to a distributed nervous system. - **AI**: This is the newest and most leveraged. Narrow AI already augments diagnosis, drug discovery, materials science, logistics optimization, scientific instrumentation, and accessibility tools (translation, vision for the blind, etc.). As capabilities scale, it offers leverage on humanity's hardest problems: protein folding, climate modeling, energy innovation, and scaling education. The risks—labor displacement, misuse, alignment failures, over-dependence—are non-trivial and require serious governance. But a pre-AI ceiling on cognitive augmentation would leave us solving 21st-century problems with 20th-century tools. Historical precedent shows new general technologies (printing press, electricity, computing) create more net capability than they destroy, even as they disrupt. Empirical trends support this: global extreme poverty, child mortality, and literacy have fallen sharply during the periods these technologies scaled. Life expectancy, material living standards, and the stock of scientific knowledge rose. These are not illusions or "just correlation." ### Real Costs and Pathologies The downsides are not trivial and explain much of the nostalgia: - Sedentary lifestyles, pollution, and traffic deaths from cars. - Attention fragmentation, social comparison, political polarization, and privacy erosion from always-on connectivity. - Potential for AI to concentrate power, automate away meaning-giving work, or create subtle forms of control. - Erosion of certain human skills (navigation, memory, basic repair) and face-to-face community. Localism, physical competence, and unmediated experience have intrinsic value. Technology is cumulative and path-dependent; we cannot painlessly revert. Humans adapt. We co-evolve with our tools. The pattern across history is that people who reject broad technological categories outright remain small minorities; the majority integrates the new capabilities while complaining about the side effects. The wiser path is to mitigate the harms (regulation, norms, education, better design) without denying the leverage. Living *entirely* without these would likely make most humans materially poorer, less healthy, less informed, and less able to solve the very problems technology itself helped create (climate, pandemics, resource constraints). The evidence favors refinement and wisdom in use over renunciation. The challenge is cultivating the character and institutions capable of wielding powerful tools without being used by them.
