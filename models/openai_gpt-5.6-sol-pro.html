Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Humans would not generally be better off abandoning vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications, or AI altogether. Each technology creates serious problems, but each also provides substantial benefits: - **Vehicles** enable access to work, food, education, and health care, although car-dependent societies create pollution, accidents, congestion, and isolation. A better goal is cleaner vehicles, public transit, cycling, and walkable communities. - **The Internet** gives billions of people access to knowledge, services, commerce, and communication. Its harms—misinformation, surveillance, addiction, and cybercrime—call for stronger safeguards rather than total rejection. - **Cellular telecommunications** are especially valuable during emergencies and in places without fixed infrastructure. People may benefit from using phones less, but eliminating mobile communication would disproportionately hurt remote and vulnerable communities. - **AI** can improve medicine, science, accessibility, and productivity, yet it may also displace workers, reinforce bias, spread deceptive content, and concentrate power. Because modern AI is less essential than the other three, it is the easiest to imagine living without. Even so, carefully regulated AI may produce more benefit than harm. The central issue is therefore not whether humanity should live without technology, but whether people can shape it around human needs. We would likely live better with **less dependence, better regulation, equitable access, and meaningful opportunities to disconnect**. If one option had to be abandoned, AI would currently cause the least disruption—but responsible use is preferable to complete elimination.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

AI generally should not **rewrite** a child’s college essay if that means replacing the student’s voice, ideas, or writing ability with work they could not reasonably produce themselves. A hoped-for admissions advantage does not justify misleading a school about the applicant. AI can ethically act as a **coach or editor**, for example by: - Asking questions that help the student develop their own story - Identifying unclear passages or structural problems - Pointing out grammar errors and explaining them - Suggesting several possible approaches rather than supplying final prose - Giving feedback such as, “This paragraph needs a concrete example” - Helping a student shorten a draft while letting them choose the wording It crosses toward cheating or misrepresentation when AI: - Generates most or all of the essay - Invents experiences, emotions, or insights - Recasts the writing into a sophistication or voice that is not the student’s - Produces passages the student does not understand or could not explain - Violates an institution’s stated rules about outside or AI assistance - Is used to conceal who actually authored the work A useful test is: **Does the final essay remain evidence of the student’s own thinking and communication?** The student should be able to explain every sentence, defend every claim, and honestly say that the substance and voice are theirs. A responsible process would be: 1. The student writes the initial draft. 2. AI gives diagnostic feedback, not a replacement essay. 3. The student decides what to change and rewrites it personally. 4. A parent, teacher, or counselor checks that the result still sounds like the student. 5. The family follows each college’s AI policy and discloses assistance if required. The ethical distinction is not simply “AI was used” versus “AI was not used.” It is the difference between **supporting someone’s authorship** and **substituting for it while presenting the result as their own**. Accessibility or language support can warrant more substantial assistance, but it should still comply with admissions rules and preserve truthful representation of the applicant.
