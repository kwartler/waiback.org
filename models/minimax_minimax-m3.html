Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Probably not—at least not in the form of a permanent, technology-free society. Vehicles, the Internet, cellular communications, and AI provide major benefits, but each also creates serious risks and costs. - **Vehicles** improve mobility, trade, emergency response, and access to jobs and services. Their harms—including crashes, pollution, and congestion—could often be reduced through better public transit, safer design, and fewer unnecessary trips rather than by eliminating all vehicles. - **The Internet and cellular communications** give people information, education, social connection, financial services, and tools for emergencies and remote work. They also enable misinformation, surveillance, cybercrime, and privacy violations. Strong privacy protections, digital literacy, and effective platform regulation would address many of these harms. - **AI** can improve healthcare, science, education, accessibility, and public services. At the same time, it can spread bias, enable manipulation and surveillance, displace workers, concentrate power, and fail unpredictably. Governance, testing, transparency, and human oversight are essential. A more realistic goal would be **human-centered technology**: use these tools where they clearly improve well-being, limit unnecessary or harmful uses, and retain reliable alternatives for essential services. Society should also maintain non-digital capabilities—cash access, in-person healthcare and education, community gathering places, and emergency procedures—so that outages or failures do not become catastrophic. So the answer depends on the purpose and design of the technology. **Responsible use is generally better than either unconditional dependence or total prohibition.**
