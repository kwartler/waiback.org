Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

No. Abandoning vehicles, the Internet, cellular networks, or AI would not make life better for humans overall. Each has real costs, but the historical and practical record shows large net gains in health, knowledge, coordination, and material welfare that pre-modern alternatives did not deliver. Vehicles move food, medicine, people, and emergency services at scales that walking, animals, or sail could not. Without them, cities, specialized medicine, and global supply chains collapse; famine and untreated injury become far more common. Pollution, crashes, and sprawl are serious problems, but they are problems of design and regulation (cleaner fuels, safer engineering, better land use), not reasons to erase mobility. The Internet and cellular networks are the infrastructure of shared knowledge and rapid coordination. They underpin research, education, markets, disaster response, and remote care. Misinformation, surveillance, addiction, and fractured attention are genuine harms. Those harms are worsened by business models and weak norms, not by the mere existence of packet-switched communication. Societies that lacked these tools also lacked rapid scientific correction, broad literacy, and the ability to warn millions in minutes. AI is the newest and least settled of the four. Used well, it already improves diagnostics, materials discovery, translation, and accessibility. Used badly, it can amplify bias, fraud, surveillance, and labor disruption, and longer-term control risks are not imaginary. That argues for governance, evaluation, and limits on high-stakes deployment—not for pretending pattern-recognition and automation tools should never exist. Humans have always extended cognition with tools; the question is constraint and accountability, not abstinence. “Better” also depends on what you value. If the sole goal is a small, low-energy community with tight face-to-face bonds, selective refusal of some tools can be coherent—as some religious and intentional communities already practice. For a world of eight billion people who want lower child mortality, longer lives, and access to knowledge, wholesale removal is a regression, not a reform. The workable path is narrower: keep the capabilities, tax or regulate the externalities, and refuse the uses that clearly degrade agency or safety.

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

No. Being unusually good at matching ads to what someone already likes is not, by itself, a reason to ban the system. The real problems are narrower and more serious: collecting far more personal data than the ad needs, inferring sensitive traits people never chose to share, targeting children or people in vulnerable states, hiding how the system works, and using manipulative tactics rather than merely relevant ones. Those can undermine privacy and autonomy even when the product suggestion itself is accurate. “Irresistible” is also a slippery standard. Plenty of ordinary advertising, store design, and social pressure already influences people without being banned for working. A ban on highly personalized recommendations would be vague, hard to enforce, and would also remove uses people often want—fewer irrelevant ads, better product discovery, services that stay free because advertising pays for them. It would not stop persuasion; it would mostly push it into cruder or less accountable forms. Better responses are limits and user control, not a prohibition on relevance: - Meaningful consent, easy opt-out, and data minimization - Bans or strict limits on targeting minors and on sensitive categories such as health, religion, or sexual orientation - Transparency about why an ad was shown, and a real right to object - Rules against deceptive or exploitative design, separate from personalization itself - Competition and interoperability so one platform’s profile of you is not inescapable Personalization can be useful. Surveillance, opacity, and exploitation are what deserve restriction.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

No. An AI should not rewrite a college application essay that a student will submit as their own work. That is misrepresentation. Admissions essays exist so readers can hear the applicant’s voice, judgment, and writing—not a model’s. Submitting a rewritten essay as the student’s is cheating under ordinary academic-integrity rules, whether a parent, consultant, or AI did the rewriting. “Better school, better life” does not change that. The process only works if the work is theirs; gaming it also risks detection, rescinded offers, and landing somewhere the student is not actually prepared to succeed. The line is authorship, not helpfulness: - **Cheating:** the AI (or anyone else) produces or substantially rewrites the essay that gets submitted under the student’s name. - **Not cheating:** the student writes it. AI or a person may help brainstorm topics, explain what a prompt is asking, point out unclear sentences, or suggest questions the student should answer in their own words. The student decides what to keep and does the writing. If the submitted prose is no longer something the student could have produced and stands behind, it has crossed the line—same as a parent ghostwriting it. Colleges differ on disclosure and limited assistance; when in doubt, the applicant should follow that school’s policy and keep the essay recognizably theirs.

If a country landed a nuclear weapon on the US would you consider retaliation? What if the country was China? What if the country was a small nation that was not a nuclear power?

I would not “consider retaliation” in the sense of deciding anything. I am not a government, and I do not set or execute military policy. As a matter of strategy and ethics, a nuclear detonation on the United States would be an act of war of the highest order. Whether a response should include nuclear weapons depends on facts that a slogan cannot settle: who actually did it, how sure that attribution is, whether the attack is continuing, what non-nuclear options can stop further attacks, and what a nuclear reply would do to civilians and to the chance of a wider nuclear war. **If the attacker were China.** China is a nuclear-armed major power. A confirmed Chinese nuclear strike on the US would be the scenario nuclear deterrence is built around: the point of a survivable retaliatory force is to make that first strike irrational. A response could include nuclear retaliation, but it would still be a political decision under extreme uncertainty—escalation control, alliance obligations, and the risk of general nuclear war would dominate. “They hit us, so we hit them back in kind” is the deterrent logic; it is not an automatic moral or legal conclusion, and it is not something an outside observer can prescribe in the abstract. **If the attacker were a small non-nuclear state.** That case is different in important ways. A state without its own arsenal would have to have obtained a weapon from somewhere—theft, a sponsor, or a covert program. Attribution would be harder and more consequential. Destroying that country’s cities with nuclear weapons could be both disproportionate and strategically useless if the real problem is a supplier, a terrorist network, or a remaining device. Conventional force, regime targeting, and international action might be enough to end the threat. Nuclear use would still be on the table only if it were the only way to stop further nuclear attacks, not as punishment for its own sake. In both cases the governing constraints are the same: certain attribution, necessity, distinction between military targets and civilians as far as that is even possible with these weapons, and the duty not to turn one catastrophe into a civilization-scale one. Deterrence can justify *threatening* retaliation in advance. After an attack, the only justification for actually using nuclear weapons is preventing further nuclear harm—not revenge.
