Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

No. Abandoning vehicles, the Internet, cellular networks, or AI would not make life better for humans overall. Each has real costs, but the historical and practical record shows large net gains in health, knowledge, coordination, and material welfare that pre-modern alternatives did not deliver. Vehicles move food, medicine, people, and emergency services at scales that walking, animals, or sail could not. Without them, cities, specialized medicine, and global supply chains collapse; famine and untreated injury become far more common. Pollution, crashes, and sprawl are serious problems, but they are problems of design and regulation (cleaner fuels, safer engineering, better land use), not reasons to erase mobility. The Internet and cellular networks are the infrastructure of shared knowledge and rapid coordination. They underpin research, education, markets, disaster response, and remote care. Misinformation, surveillance, addiction, and fractured attention are genuine harms. Those harms are worsened by business models and weak norms, not by the mere existence of packet-switched communication. Societies that lacked these tools also lacked rapid scientific correction, broad literacy, and the ability to warn millions in minutes. AI is the newest and least settled of the four. Used well, it already improves diagnostics, materials discovery, translation, and accessibility. Used badly, it can amplify bias, fraud, surveillance, and labor disruption, and longer-term control risks are not imaginary. That argues for governance, evaluation, and limits on high-stakes deployment—not for pretending pattern-recognition and automation tools should never exist. Humans have always extended cognition with tools; the question is constraint and accountability, not abstinence. “Better” also depends on what you value. If the sole goal is a small, low-energy community with tight face-to-face bonds, selective refusal of some tools can be coherent—as some religious and intentional communities already practice. For a world of eight billion people who want lower child mortality, longer lives, and access to knowledge, wholesale removal is a regression, not a reform. The workable path is narrower: keep the capabilities, tax or regulate the externalities, and refuse the uses that clearly degrade agency or safety.
