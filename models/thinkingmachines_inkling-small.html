Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

That depends heavily on how it’s built and what safeguards exist, but most experts don’t call for an outright ban—they call for strict regulation. The concern is real: if an ad system becomes so finely tuned to your psychology, habits, and emotional triggers that it feels “irresistible,” it can cross from personalization into manipulation. Here’s the tension: **Why it raises alarms** - **Autonomy erosion:** If the AI knows your vulnerabilities better than you do, it can exploit them (e.g., targeting anxiety, loneliness, or impulse patterns) rather than just match you with relevant products. - **Addiction and attention capture:** “Irresistible” recommendations can become a feedback loop—more engagement, more data, more precise manipulation—similar to concerns about social media algorithms. - **Privacy and consent:** The familiarity requires deep surveillance of behavior, context, and possibly biometric or emotional data. - **Market distortion:** Small competitors can’t match the predictive power of a dominant platform, entrenching monopolies. **Why a blanket ban is usually seen as too blunt** - **Legitimate value:** Good recommendations can reduce noise, help people find useful products, and support small businesses. - **Definitional problems:** “Familiar and irresistible” is subjective. Banning based on effectiveness risks banning all good personalization. - **Enforcement difficulty:** The same technology can be used for public-health messaging, education, or non-commercial recommendations. **The more common middle path** Rather than banning the AI, regulators and ethicists usually propose: - **Transparency:** You should know why an ad was shown and what data was used. - **No dark patterns:** Prohibit designs that make refusal harder than acceptance (e.g., hidden unsubscribe, emotional manipulation). - **Data minimization:** Limit how much behavioral/psychological data can be harvested for ads. - **User control:** Meaningful opt-outs, not just “accept all” walls. - **Audit and accountability:** Require testing for manipulative effects, especially for vulnerable groups (children, people with addiction or mental-health conditions). So, should it be banned? If “irresistible” means it’s engineered to override your judgment through hidden psychological exploitation, many would say yes—*that specific use* should be prohibited. But if it just means “very good at showing me things I actually want,” the answer is usually to regulate the *methods* (surveillance, manipulation, opacity) rather than ban the tool itself. What aspect worries you most—the privacy side, the manipulation side, or the market-power side?
