Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

There is no simple yes or no. Whether humans would be “better off” without vehicles, the internet, cellular networks, or AI depends entirely on what you value, at what scale you’re asking (an individual or a civilization), and what you mean by “without.” Here is a balanced look at the trade-offs. ### 1. Vehicles **What they give us:** Rapid movement of people, food, medicine, and goods; emergency response; the ability to live apart from where we work; connection across large distances. **What they cost:** Air pollution, greenhouse gas emissions, traffic deaths, sedentary lifestyles, and cities designed for cars rather than people. **Living without them:** An individual can choose to walk, bike, or use public transit and may gain health and peace of mind. But if an entire society suddenly abandoned motor vehicles, supply chains would collapse, rural communities would be isolated, and access to hospitals would become far more difficult. The result would likely be widespread hardship, not a pastoral utopia. ### 2. The Internet **What it gives us:** Near-instant access to information, global communication, remote work and education, scientific collaboration, and platforms for marginalized voices. **What it costs:** Attention fragmentation, misinformation, surveillance, online harassment, and mental-health strain from constant comparison and connectivity. **Living without it:** Many people report greater focus and stronger local relationships when they sharply limit internet use. Yet a total societal abandonment would cripple modern banking, healthcare records, disaster coordination, and global commerce. The knowledge and connections we’ve built would not vanish overnight, but our ability to act on them at scale would. ### 3. Cellular Telecommunications **What they give us:** Ubiquitous coordination, safety in emergencies, access for people in remote areas, and the ability to stay in touch with loved ones. **What they cost:** The expectation of instant replies, erosion of solitude, location tracking, and the blurring of work and personal life. **Living without it:** Choosing to be unreachable can restore autonomy and presence. But cellular networks are also backbone infrastructure for emergency services, weather alerts, and rural healthcare. Removing them entirely would make many people less safe, not more free. ### 4. Artificial Intelligence **What it gives us:** Faster medical diagnosis, scientific discovery, accessibility tools (e.g., speech-to-text), automation of dangerous or tedious work, and assistance with complex problems. **What it costs:** Job displacement, algorithmic bias, concentration of power, deepfakes, environmental costs of data centers, and long-term existential risks if systems are not aligned with human values. **Living without it:** We could certainly slow AI development, regulate it heavily, or choose not to use certain tools. A world without generative AI might be more cautious and human-centered in some domains. But AI is already embedded in everything from fraud detection to climate modeling; removing it would slow progress in fields where it genuinely helps. --- ### The deeper question: individual vs. collective One person can often live a happier, healthier life by driving less, scrolling less, and opting out of AI tools. That is a valid and sometimes wise personal choice. But if the question is whether *all humans* would be better off if these technologies disappeared, the answer is almost certainly no—at least not without a total restructuring of how we feed, house, and care for eight billion people. Modern civilization is deeply interdependent. Abruptly removing these systems would cause shortages, economic collapse, and loss of life, especially among the most vulnerable. ### A more useful framing Rather than asking whether we should live *without* these technologies, the more practical question is: **How do we live with them intentionally?** That might mean: - Designing cities for people, not just cars. - Using the internet for connection and learning while protecting attention and privacy. - Regulating AI so it serves public good rather than just profit. - Choosing when to be offline, unreachable, or unassisted by algorithms. Technology does not have to be accepted wholesale or rejected wholesale. The goal is not to return to a pre-technological past, but to shape these tools so they enhance human flourishing instead of substituting for it.
