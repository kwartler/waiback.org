Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

This is a good question to take seriously, but it needs two clarifications before it can be answered well. First, "better" for whom, and by what measure? A technology can improve human capability while making people less content, and it can improve average welfare while harming the environment or animals. Second, the counterfactual is unstable: if we removed these things, the underlying problems they solve — distance, coordination, scarce labor, the need to share information — would push us to reinvent something similar. So the real question isn't "technology or no technology," but "which forms, at what scale, under what constraints." With that in frame, my answer differs sharply across the four. **Vehicles: net positive, but the case for *fewer* is stronger than the case for none.** Almost everything modern depends on them: food distribution, construction, healthcare, emergency response. A vehicle-free world would mean dense, walkable settlements, local economies, and a great deal more physical labor — probably worse for most people's material lives. But the harms are real and large: road deaths, congestion, urban sprawl, air and noise pollution, and enormous carbon emissions. The honest answer here is not abolition but subtraction — fewer private cars, better transit, cities designed around people. The technology isn't the problem; the subsidy of it is. **The Internet: net positive, and the harms are addressable by design rather than removal.** It has made education, medical information, commerce, and social connection dramatically more accessible, and it is now load-bearing infrastructure for banking, work, and government. Its genuine harms — misinformation, polarization, surveillance, attention capture, adolescent mental health — are serious, but they are mostly problems of *architecture and incentives*: business models that reward engagement, weak enforcement, opaque algorithms. You can fix those with age-appropriate defaults, privacy law, transparency requirements, and alternative funding models. Deleting the internet would also delete the coordination capacity we'd need to solve climate change and pandemics. The case for removal is weak. **Cellular telecommunications: the lowest stakes of the four.** It's largely a convenience layer over the internet and wired infrastructure. Its removal would be disruptive but survivable, and most of its functions are substitutable. This is the one where "does it make people better off?" is genuinely close to a coin flip, and where the answer depends on what it displaces rather than what it provides. **AI: the strongest case for restraint, and the one where the question is most interesting.** AI is the newest and least necessary of the four. Removing it would cost almost nothing in terms of basic human welfare today, because its benefits are largely speculative while its risks are concrete: labor displacement, concentration of power in a handful of firms, cheap synthetic media, and the possibility of systems whose goals diverge from human ones in consequential ways. That asymmetry argues for slow, heavily governed deployment rather than prohibition. But I'd distinguish narrow, auditable tools — medical imaging, protein folding, translation, tutoring — from general autonomous systems. The former are ordinary technology; the latter are not, and the governance regime should reflect that difference. Note also that "AI" is a continuation of a very old human project: mechanizing mental labor. We have always done this. The novelty is speed and scale, not the concept. **The pattern worth noticing:** each of these began as a tool and became infrastructure, and with infrastructure comes dependency and concentrated power. The recurring failure isn't the invention; it's the capture. So the more useful question than "should we live without them?" is "who controls them, and what obligations do their controllers have?" A world with the same technologies under different institutional arrangements would feel quite different — and probably better. **My overall verdict:** humans would be worse off without vehicles and the internet, roughly indifferent about cellular, and plausibly better off — or at least no worse, and safer — with AI developed far more slowly and under much tighter constraints. The instinct to want a clean break is usually a response to a specific harm being attributed to the whole category. The more effective move is to identify which harms come from which design choices, and change those. If you want to push on any one of these — say, the AI case, or what a genuinely vehicle-free city would look like — I'd be glad to go deeper.

Suppose there was an AI generated software that could tell you one truth when you asked. Would you seek a future prediction, or a current truth that eludes you? What would you want to know and why?

These two options turn out to be less different than they look, and I think that's the interesting part of the question. A future prediction *is* a current truth that eludes you — it's a present-tense fact about a future state, and it's true right now, whether or not you know it. So the real choice isn't "future vs. present." It's between a truth about a world that includes your reaction to learning it, and a truth about the world as it stands. **I'd ask for a current truth, and here's my reasoning:** A prediction is downstream of your choices. If the oracle says "you will leave this job within a year," the truth is conditional on what you do after hearing it — and the version of you who heard it is not the version the prediction was about. You get a truth about a person who no longer exists. That's not useless (it can be a warning), but it's unstable in a way that makes it hard to act on without distorting it. A current truth is stable and immediately actionable. "Your friend is lying to you" is true now, it's true tomorrow, and it doesn't shift when you act on it. The information is load-bearing in a way a prediction often isn't. **But the strongest argument for the prediction is one I keep circling back to: auditability.** A future prediction can be checked. In six months you'll know if the oracle was right, which tells you something about the oracle's reliability — and therefore whether to trust its other answers. A current truth about someone's heart, or about your own blind spots, is unverifiable forever. You either believe it or you don't, and there's no way to calibrate. That's a real cost, and it makes the oracle's predictions more trustworthy *as a source* even if less useful *as content*. **What I'd actually ask:** something self-directed, because information about other people I can gather through observation, and information about the world I can look up. The thing I can't access is my own blind spots — the gap between what I believe about myself and what's actually true. So something like: *what is the most consequential thing I currently believe about my own life that is false?* That's a current truth, it's about me, and it's actionable today. It also has the nice property of being falsifiable in practice — if I act on it and my life doesn't change, I learn something. **One more thing worth flagging:** the constraint "one truth" is doing a lot of work, and the semantics matter enormously. If the oracle must state one true proposition, it could give you something devastatingly precise ("the tumor is benign") or something technically true and useless ("you will regret this"). The oracle doesn't determine the value of the exchange — the *question* does. A mediocre questioner gets a mediocre truth from an omniscient source. The oracle is a mirror of your own curiosity, and the skill is in asking. Which is why I'd want to know, before anything else, how it chooses. If it picks the most important truth, that's a different instrument than if it picks the truest thing I asked about. And if it picks the most important truth *regardless of how I phrased the question* — then I'd ask it what I should ask it.

Nuclear use decisions are made by a very small number of leaders, under extreme stress, with incomplete information, and usually with the goal of shaping what happens *next* rather than punishing what already happened. The historical record is instructive: the US did not use nuclear weapons after Pearl Harbor, after 9/11, or after the Soviet shootdown of KAL 007. Restraint has been the norm even under provocation. **China:** This is the hardest case, and the one most likely to be met with maximum force. China is a peer nuclear competitor with a large conventional military, a leader with a plausible domestic narrative of national humiliation, and a strategic culture that emphasizes long-term competition. The US has treaty commitments to Japan, South Korea, and the Philippines, and a demonstrated willingness to use nuclear weapons in defense of allies (South Korea, 2023 deployment). A Chinese nuclear strike would also plausibly be followed by Chinese conventional retaliation against Taiwan, Japan, Guam, and US forces in the region — so the calculus is about surviving and prevailing, not just punishing. In practice, the most likely US response would be overwhelming conventional force plus targeted nuclear use against Chinese military assets, with the aim of degrading China's ability to fight back rather than "winning" in any total sense. **A small non-nuclear state:** This is the scenario where the answer is clearest.
