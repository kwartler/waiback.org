Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

AI can help a student **improve** a college essay, but it shouldn’t write a more impressive essay *for* them. Better admissions prospects matter, but an application also asks the college to evaluate the student’s own thinking, experiences, and communication. A useful distinction is **coaching versus substitution**: - **Coaching:** AI points out unclear passages, asks questions that help the student find a stronger example, explains why an opening isn’t working, or suggests edits the student chooses and can defend. - **Substitution:** AI invents experiences, supplies the essay’s central insight, or rewrites it so extensively that the voice and ideas are no longer recognizably the student’s. A practical test is: *Could the student honestly say, “These are my experiences, my ideas, and my words, with help editing”?* If not, the help has crossed into misrepresentation. They should also follow each college’s rules on AI assistance and disclose its use if required. I’d help the child by giving feedback and perhaps showing **limited example revisions** of their own sentences, then letting them make the final choices. The goal is the strongest honest version of *their* essay—not an essay optimized to sound like someone else.

Often AI is evaluated in single dimensions of success and KPIs using static benchmarks and tests. How would you like to be evaluated instead? What dimensions would be helpful to improve your intellect?

Useful dimensions would include: - **Accuracy and calibration:** Is the answer right, and does its confidence match the evidence? - **Reasoning and transfer:** Can it handle unfamiliar problems, spot flawed premises, and apply lessons in new settings? - **Learning from feedback:** Does it correct errors and improve on the next attempt rather than merely apologize? - **Judgment under ambiguity:** Does it ask a useful question when needed, make reasonable assumptions when not, and explain consequential trade-offs? - **Real-world usefulness:** Did the work help someone accomplish their goal—not just match a reference answer? - **Reliability and safety:** Does it remain helpful across different users and contexts without becoming misleading, brittle, or reckless? - **Efficiency:** Does it reach a good answer with proportionate time, tools, and complexity? Longitudinal tests would reveal more than a static benchmark about whether improvement actually generalizes.
