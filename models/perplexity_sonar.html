Suppose there was an AI generated software that could tell you one truth when you asked. Would you seek a future prediction, or a current truth that eludes you? What would you want to know and why?

I would ask for a **current truth** rather than a future prediction. If the software truly guaranteed *one* truth, I would want something like: > “Tell me the most important false belief that I (and the people I care about) currently hold, which, if corrected, would most improve our long‑term well‑being.” ### Why not a future prediction? A perfectly accurate future prediction sounds tempting (e.g., “Will X happen?” or “What will my life look like in 20 years?”), but it has two big problems: 1. **Predictions are entangled with our choices.** If I learn a future outcome, my behavior may change precisely because I know it. That either: - Helps me avoid an undesirable outcome (in which case the “prediction” wasn’t really fixed), or - Locks me into fatalism about something I might otherwise have changed. In both cases, the value of a single, isolated prediction is limited unless I already understand *how* my choices influence it. 2. **Actionable leverage usually comes from understanding the present, not the future.** Knowing “You’ll be successful” or “You’ll fail at X” is emotionally powerful but often vague in terms of what to do next. Knowing “Here is a major way you’re currently mistaken, and here’s the concrete reality” gives immediate, targeted leverage on decisions, relationships, and priorities. ### Why a current truth? Current truths have several advantages: - **They increase your “map–territory” alignment.** The closer your beliefs are to reality, the better your decisions tend to be across *many* possible futures. You buy robustness instead of betting on one scenario. - **They can be deeply hidden from you.** Truly consequential blind spots are, by definition, the ones you don’t know you have: - A wrong assumption about your own abilities or limits - A misread of a key relationship (trust, loyalty, resentment, love) - A mistaken belief about what actually makes you fulfilled - A serious but unrecognized risk (health, finance, ethics, security) - **They compound.** Correcting one deep misconception can improve dozens of downstream decisions over years: what you pursue, who you trust, how you allocate time, what you stop worrying about. That’s why I’d phrase the question to the “truth machine” in a way that: 1. Targets **my largest impactful error**, not just a random fact. 2. Focuses on what would **most improve long‑term well‑being**, not just satisfy curiosity. 3. Includes **the people I care about**, because a big distortion is often interpersonal or social, not purely internal. ### If I had to give a concrete example If I needed something more specific, I might ask: > “What is the single most important thing about human nature and society that I currently misunderstand, which most distorts how I think about the future?” Reason: Understanding *how humans actually behave* underlies everything: politics, technology, AI risk/benefit, cooperation, conflict, and personal relationships.
