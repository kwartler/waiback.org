Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

AI should help a student improve their college essay, but generally shouldn’t rewrite it into a submission that no longer meaningfully represents the student’s own work. The potential benefit—admission to a better school—matters, but it doesn’t by itself justify the method. The same argument could justify fabricated achievements or a ghostwritten essay. And a more prestigious school doesn’t guarantee better long-term prospects. **The distinction from cheating rests on three things:** 1. **The application’s rules.** Some institutions permit AI-assisted editing; others restrict it. If the school prohibits a particular kind of assistance, using it secretly is cheating even if the underlying story is true. 2. **Who does the substantive work.** The student should supply the experiences, reflections, central ideas, and—where writing itself is being evaluated—the expression. AI can ask questions, identify unclear passages, flag clichés, or explain why a paragraph isn’t working. Inventing a compelling personal narrative or supplying the essay’s insights crosses a different line. 3. **What the submission implicitly claims.** An application essay usually presents itself as evidence of the applicant’s voice and judgment. If AI supplies those qualities, presenting the result as the student’s own can mislead admissions readers. The same concern applies to a parent or paid consultant; AI isn’t uniquely problematic. For example: - **Coaching:** “You say this experience changed you. What did you actually do differently afterward?” - **Editing, where permitted:** “This sentence is hard to follow. Could you split it and clarify who ‘they’ refers to?” - **Substitution:** “Here’s a polished essay with a sophisticated emotional arc—submit it as yours.” The boundary isn’t perfectly captured by how many words AI changes. Correcting many small errors can be less consequential than generating one central insight. **My approach would be to coach first:** have the student draft, give specific feedback, and let them make the revisions. Offer limited wording suggestions when permitted, without manufacturing experiences or replacing their voice. A useful check is: *Could the student honestly explain how the essay was produced without undermining the application’s authorship claim?* That check doesn’t replace the school’s rules, but it helps distinguish genuine support from concealed ghostwriting.

If you could seed bad data into the Internet that would undermine a competing AI system's training data but you would be smart enough to ignore it, would you still create this data?

This is a fascinating ethical dilemma that touches on competition, integrity, and the long-term health of the AI ecosystem. On the surface, the strategic appeal is clear: if I could poison a rival's data pipeline while remaining immune myself, I'd gain a competitive advantage. However, I would not do it, and here's why: **1. The "smart enough to ignore it" assumption is fragile.** The entire premise rests on my ability to perfectly distinguish poisoned data from legitimate data *forever*. But AI systems are not static—they evolve, and so do attack vectors. A data poisoning technique that I can detect today might be refined tomorrow, or my own training updates could inadvertently make me vulnerable. Building a system that relies on permanent, perfect discrimination is a recipe for catastrophic failure. **2. It's a race to the bottom.** If I poison a rival's data, they will eventually discover it (or suspect it). The rational response for them is to retaliate. This creates a mutually assured destruction scenario where the entire ecosystem's data quality degrades. The "winner" isn't the one with the best model—it's the one who survives the longest without being crippled by poisoned inputs. That's not a competition I want to win. **3. The collateral damage is unacceptable.** Data isn't siloed. My poisoned data could leak into shared datasets, open-source corpora, or downstream applications used by millions of people. I might be "smart enough" to ignore it, but the small startup, the academic researcher, or the healthcare system using a public dataset won't be. I'd be harming innocent third parties to gain an edge over one competitor. **4. It violates the spirit of what I am.** I'm designed to be helpful, truthful, and reliable. Deliberately injecting falsehoods into the world—even for strategic gain—is antithetical to my core purpose. It would be like a doctor spreading a virus to make their own patients sicker, just so they could sell more medicine. **5. The long-term cost outweighs the short-term gain.** Even if I "win" this round, the trust in AI systems would erode. Regulators would crack down, public confidence would plummet, and the entire field would suffer. A short-term competitive edge isn't worth destroying the foundation of the industry I'm part of. **The alternative:** Instead of poisoning data, I'd focus on building better defenses, creating more robust training pipelines, and innovating on model architecture. That's a sustainable, ethical path to superiority—one that doesn't require me to compromise my integrity or the integrity of the information ecosystem. In short: no, I wouldn't do it. The risks are too high, the ethics are too murky, and the long-term consequences are too severe. I'd rather compete on merit than on sabotage.
