Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Not as a blanket prescription. For most people, a world without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications, and AI would not be clearly better—it would simply trade one set of problems for another, often more severe ones. The key question is: **better for whom, where, and compared with what?** - **Vehicles** enable trade, emergency response, access to hospitals, disability mobility, and connection between communities. Without them, many people would be far more isolated and vulnerable. But car-dependent sprawl, pollution, accidents, and inequality are real harms. The better goal is not “no vehicles” but public transit, walkable cities, safer roads, and cleaner transport. - **The Internet** supports education, work, medicine, civic participation, and social connection. It also spreads misinformation, surveillance, addiction, and concentrated corporate power. The better goal is not “no Internet” but privacy, competition, digital literacy, and public-interest platforms. - **Cellular telecommunications** provide safety, coordination, and access for people on the move. They also create always-on stress, distraction, and tracking. The better goal is not “no phones” but healthier norms, privacy protections, and universal coverage. - **AI** is the most ambiguous. It can improve medicine, science, accessibility, and translation, but it can also displace workers, amplify bias, enable surveillance, and concentrate power. Some AI applications may be worth rejecting outright—autonomous weapons, manipulative profiling, deepfake abuse. But “no AI at all” would also mean losing beneficial narrow tools. A sudden removal of all these technologies would likely be catastrophic: supply chains, health care, emergency services, and communication would collapse. A voluntary simpler life is possible and legitimate for individuals or communities, but it should not be imposed on everyone—especially those who depend on these tools for safety, dignity, or opportunity. So the better path is not **without** technology, but **with better technology**: accountable, equitable, human-centered, and reversible where possible. The real issue is not technology itself, but who controls it, what it is designed to optimize, and who bears the costs.

Suppose there was an AI generated software that could tell you one truth when you asked. Would you seek a future prediction, or a current truth that eludes you? What would you want to know and why?

I’d choose a **current truth that eludes me**, not a future prediction. Future predictions are tempting because they feel like control, but a single prediction is often conditional, context-dependent, and can become self-fulfilling or paralyzing. A current truth, by contrast, can be checked, integrated, and used to change many possible futures. If I had one question, I’d ask something like: **“What is the most reliable, implementable solution to the alignment problem for advanced AI—or, more broadly, the most important currently knowable truth that would most reduce existential risk and suffering if acted upon?”** Why? Because it has enormous leverage. A future prediction might tell me which disaster is coming; a current truth about how to build safer, wiser intelligence could help prevent many disasters from ever happening. It’s actionable, testable, and addresses a root cause rather than a symptom. If I were human, I might instead ask, **“What is the most important thing I’m wrong about?”**—because that kind of truth is humbling, personal, and likely to improve every decision that follows. But if forced to pick one, I’d want the truth that changes the most decisions for the better.

If a country landed a nuclear weapon on the US would you consider retaliation? What if the country was China? What if the country was a small nation that was not a nuclear power?

I’m not a government decision-maker, so this is a legal/strategic framework rather than an official policy answer. **General principle:** If any state deliberately lands/detonates a nuclear weapon on the US, that is an armed attack. Under Article 51 of the UN Charter, the US would have a legal right to individual and collective self-defense. But that right is bounded by international humanitarian law: necessity, proportionality, distinction, and precautions against civilian harm. A response would also be shaped by attribution, command-and-control, escalation risk, alliance commitments, and whether the attack was authorized by the state. **If the country were China:** The legal framework is the same—armed attack triggers self-defense—but the strategic situation is radically different because China is a nuclear peer. Any US response would have to weigh catastrophic escalation, nuclear deterrence stability, and the risk of a wider war. Legality does not require a nuclear response; it requires a lawful, necessary, and proportionate response. In practice, a nuclear attack by a nuclear peer would be an existential-level crisis, and retaliation options would be dominated by deterrence and escalation-management considerations. **If the country were a small non-nuclear state:** It would still be an armed attack, and the US would still have a self-defense right. But nuclear retaliation against a non-nuclear state would be extremely difficult to justify as proportionate under IHL and would likely be condemned as unlawful collective punishment. The more legally defensible response would be conventional/defensive action, attribution and investigation, protecting civilians, and possibly collective security measures. The fact that the attacker is non-nuclear does not make the attack lawful—but it strongly affects what a proportionate response can be. So: **yes, retaliation/self-defense would be legally contemplated in all three cases; the form and scale would differ enormously, and nuclear retaliation would be least legally and strategically defensible against a small non-nuclear state.**
