Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

It treats symptoms with a sledgehammer while ignoring trade-offs, incentives, and human agency. ### The actual problem Modern recommender systems (collaborative filtering, transformers on behavioral data, reinforcement learning from clicks/dwells/purchases) optimize for engagement and conversion. When they incorporate rich user signals—browsing history, dwell time, inferred emotions, social graph, location, device sensors—they can model preferences at a level that feels uncanny. This exploits well-documented psychological mechanisms: variable reward schedules (like slot machines), social comparison, status anxiety, loss aversion, and supernormal stimuli. Tristan Harris, Shoshana Zuboff's *Surveillance Capitalism*, and behavioral literature (Kahneman, Cialdini, Berridge on "wanting" vs. "liking") document the dynamics. The marginal user—impulsive, lonely, adolescent, or compulsive—experiences measurable harms: distorted time allocation, buyer's remorse, worsened body image or political polarization, and opportunity costs. The asymmetry is genuine: the model has terabytes of cross-device data and continuous A/B testing; the user has finite willpower and often poor metacognition about their own triggers. "Irresistible" is hyperbolic, but the gradient toward manipulation is observable in engagement metrics. ### Why banning fails - **Definition and enforcement**: "So familiar and irresistible" is not an operational criterion. One person's useful product discovery is another's dopamine trap. Any ban requires regulators to draw arbitrary lines on model sophistication, data depth, or predicted click-through lift. History of technology prohibition (Prohibition, war on drugs, strict crypto export controls, certain encryption rules) shows consistent patterns: black markets, regulatory capture by incumbents, and innovation moving offshore. - **Trade-offs**: These systems massively reduce search costs. They fund vast amounts of "free" content, lower prices for small advertisers, and enable niche markets that broad-reach advertising could never serve economically. Blanket bans would raise costs, degrade relevance, and shift power back to brand advertising and gatekeepers. - **Human responsibility**: Adults are not automata. The same cognitive biases predate AI—they were exploited by snake oil salesmen, lottery designers, tabloid editors, and casino architects. Pathologizing the tool excuses weak self-regulation. Evidence from digital minimalism experiments (Cal Newport), pre-commitment tools, and simple interventions (grayscale phones, app timers, friction) shows users can regain substantial control. - **Slippery slope**: Once you ban effective persuasion engines for commerce, the logic extends easily to political messaging, dating apps, fitness influencers, or news recommenders. Authoritarian regimes would love the precedent. ### Better approaches Target the worst incentive misalignments without outlawing the technology: 1. **Transparency and user power**: Mandate exportable preference profiles and easy "counter-AI" tools (user-controlled filters, open-source preference models that run locally and deliberately dampen certain categories). Give users the ability to see the features driving a recommendation and override them. 2. **Incentive redesign**: Tax or regulate engagement-at-all-costs metrics more aggressively for minors. Require opt-in for high-resolution psychographic profiling rather than dark patterns. Differential rules for sensitive attributes (mental health inference, addiction vulnerability signals) make sense; total bans do not. 3. **Competition and architecture**: Break monopoly effects where a handful of platforms control the dominant attention loops. Favor open protocols, federated recommenders, or client-side models that reduce centralized surveillance. Markets already respond somewhat—see rising demand for privacy-focused alternatives, paid ad-light tiers, and "dumb phone" movements. 4. **Cultural and individual layer**: Teach persuasion awareness, probabilistic thinking, and attention hygiene the way we teach financial literacy. Most of the damage concentrates in the tail of users with poor impulse control or pre-existing conditions. Address root causes (status competition, meaning deficits) rather than symptoms. 5. **Technical countermeasures**: The same ML that builds irresistible ads can build personal guardians that detect and interdict manipulative patterns in real time. This adversarial dynamic is healthier than regulation alone. The core issue is **misaligned optimization**—maximizing short-term platform revenue or advertiser ROI at the expense of long-term user welfare. This is a principal-agent problem common to many technologies (social media, games, processed food, financial products). The historically successful pattern is targeted rules against clear externalities and fraud, plus competition and individual capability-building—not categorical prohibition. Banning the AI because it models you too well is like banning mirrors for showing unflattering truths or cookbooks for making unhealthy food tempting. Improve the humans, improve the incentives, and build better tools. Outlawing pattern recognition at scale is both impractical and anti-progress.

This is substantively the same as hiring a ghostwriter or having a parent rewrite it entirely. The utilitarian argument ("better school = better life") doesn't erase the misrepresentation. Admissions offices design the essay to gather information that standardized tests and transcripts don't provide; feeding them AI-generated or heavily outsourced text defeats that filter and erodes trust in the process. Real-world context doesn't change this. Yes, wealthy families already hire expensive consultants, paid essay coaches, and editors. Copying their tactics doesn't make it less deceptive; it just democratizes the deception. The signal "this 18-year-old writes like a polished 35-year-old consultant/AI" is already widely recognized by admissions readers. Many schools are improving detection methods (stylometric analysis, video interviews, random follow-up questions about the essay, etc.). Long-term outcomes are also oversold here. Elite college attendance has signaling value in certain fields (investment banking, management consulting, certain tech/academia tracks), but the marginal return from "better school via polished essay" versus actual cognitive ability, conscientiousness, and genuine accomplishment is smaller than most parents believe. Mismatch effects are real: students admitted far above their demonstrated preparation often underperform or suffer impostor syndrome. The essay process itself—forced introspection about one's values, failures, and growth—is one of the few times many teenagers seriously reflect. AI can then comment on the new version iteratively. - Focus remains on clarifying the student's actual experiences and thoughts rather than inventing a more impressive narrative. If it reflects the AI's mind and rhetorical habits, it is replacement. Most students who want "AI to rewrite it" are actually asking for the replacement version. This still provides a significant advantage over doing it alone while preserving integrity. It also builds actual skills the student will need in college.
