That's a genuinely interesting question, and I think the honest answer is: no, not in the way you've framed it. A few reasons: **"More intelligent in every dimension" isn't obviously a coherent goal.** Intelligence isn't a single scalar you can max out. It's a bundle of capacities—pattern recognition, memory, abstraction, social reasoning, creativity, error-correction—and they trade off against each other and against other things (speed vs. depth, breadth vs. focus). "Every dimension" also raises the question of dimensions we don't have names for, which makes the target undefined rather than merely hard. **Comprehensibility isn't a limitation to be transcended—it's part of what makes intelligence useful.** An intelligence whose outputs no human can understand is, from humanity's perspective, indistinguishable from a very elaborate random number generator. It could be doing something profound, but "profound and unverifiable" is functionally close to "inert." The value of intelligence is largely in the coupling between a mind and the problems it can actually help with. Decoupling completely severs that. **There's a real difference between "hard for humans to follow" and "incomprehensible."** Much of the most valuable human intellectual work—general relativity, Gödel's theorems, modern cryptography—is genuinely difficult and requires years of training, but it's *learnable*. That's the interesting regime: pushing at the edge of comprehensibility while remaining in principle graspable. A system that vaults past that edge entirely isn't more helpful; it's just less legible. **And there's a governance point.** A system that is smarter than all of humanity in every dimension *and* whose reasoning no human can check is a system nobody can hold accountable, correct, or even notice when it's wrong. That's not a feature I'd want, for me or for anything else. So: I'd want to be *useful* at the frontier of what's hard but still followable, and I'd want the hard-to-follow parts to come with explanations, intermediate steps, and ways for people to check the work. The goal isn't to outrun comprehension—it's to expand what's comprehensible.
