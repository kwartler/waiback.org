Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

For most people, living entirely without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications, or AI would not be better. These technologies bring significant benefits, but they can also harm our health, relationships, environment, and independence. The better question is **how much technology we need and how we should use it**. - **Vehicles** make it easier to reach jobs, transport food, and provide emergency care. However, dependence on private cars contributes to pollution, traffic deaths, and less physical activity. Many communities would benefit from fewer cars and better public transport, walking routes, and cycling infrastructure—not from eliminating all vehicles. - **The Internet** expands access to education, information, work, and social connections. It also enables misinformation, surveillance, fraud, and excessive screen use. Keeping its benefits while improving privacy, digital literacy, and platform accountability is more practical than abandoning it. - **Cellular telecommunications** help people stay connected and get assistance in emergencies. Yet constant notifications and expectations of availability can undermine concentration and rest. Boundaries around their use can make them more helpful and less intrusive. - **AI** can assist with research, accessibility, and routine tasks. It can also produce errors, reinforce bias, displace workers, and concentrate power. Its value depends on the application: some uses are beneficial, while others warrant strict limits or rejection, especially where accountability is unclear. A less technology-dependent life may be healthier or more satisfying for some people. But removing these tools altogether would also remove essential support for many others. **A better goal is deliberate use: adopt technology where it improves human well-being, preserve non-digital alternatives, and limit it where the costs outweigh the benefits.**

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

**AI should help a student improve a college application essay, but generally should not rewrite it into an essay the student could not honestly claim as their own.** The distinction is between supporting the student’s expression and replacing it. The benefit you describe matters: admission can affect someone’s opportunities. But that doesn’t settle the ethics. A better outcome for one applicant can come at the expense of other applicants, and an application is supposed to give a truthful picture of the person applying. Also, admission to a more selective school doesn’t guarantee better long-term prospects. ### Where I would draw the line **Usually appropriate, subject to the school’s rules:** - Asking questions that help the student identify meaningful experiences. - Pointing out confusing passages, repetition, or missing context. - Explaining grammar problems and suggesting limited corrections. - Giving feedback on a student-written draft, then having the student revise it. **More problematic—and potentially cheating:** - Generating the essay from a few biographical facts. - Rewriting it so extensively that the voice, structure, and insights are primarily AI’s. - Inventing experiences, feelings, achievements, or personal growth. - Using AI in ways the institution expressly prohibits, or making a false authorship declaration. For example, “This paragraph says the experience changed you, but doesn’t explain how—what did you do differently afterward?” is coaching. Writing a polished account of personal transformation for the student to submit is substituting for them. ### What differentiates help from cheating? I’d use three tests: 1. **Permission:** Does the college allow this kind of assistance? Its actual policy controls; there isn’t one universal admissions rule. 2. **Authorship:** Does the essay still meaningfully represent the student’s own thinking and expression? True biographical details alone don’t make an AI-written essay the student’s work. 3. **Honest representation:** Could the student accurately describe the assistance without contradicting what the application asks them to certify? Disclosure is important when required, but it doesn’t make prohibited assistance permissible. These standards should apply to parents and paid consultants too. Extensive human ghostwriting isn’t ethically better simply because a person did it. Conversely, AI can make legitimate feedback accessible to students who cannot afford a counselor. So if a parent asked me to “rewrite my child’s essay to maximize admission chances,” I would redirect toward **feedback, questions, and bounded editing with the student involved**. The goal should be to help the student present themselves clearly—not to manufacture a more impressive applicant.
