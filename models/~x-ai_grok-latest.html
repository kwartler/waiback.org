Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

No. For humanity as a whole, abandoning vehicles, the internet, cellular networks, or AI would make life worse on net, not better—though each brings real costs that need managing. These technologies are not optional luxuries layered on an otherwise stable baseline. Modern food systems, medicine, disaster response, and scientific coordination depend on mechanized transport and real-time communication. Removing vehicles would collapse long-distance logistics that feed cities and move patients and supplies; pre-industrial mortality and famine patterns are the historical reference, not a romantic alternative. The internet and cellular networks similarly underpin education, markets, emergency alerts, and the diffusion of knowledge at a scale no prior medium matched. Their downsides—misinformation, surveillance incentives, attention capture, unequal access—are serious, but the counterfactual (slower coordination, more isolated expertise, weaker early-warning systems) is worse for most people. AI is newer and its balance sheet is still open. It already accelerates useful work in areas like protein modeling, materials screening, and language access, and it will keep doing so. It also concentrates power, can amplify errors or bias at scale, and raises legitimate questions about labor displacement and long-term control. Those are reasons for governance, evaluation, and restraint—not for pretending the capability can or should be unwound while everything else stays modern. Selective non-use (as some communities practice) can be personally fulfilling; it does not scale to eight billion people whose calories, vaccines, and infrastructure already assume industrial and digital systems. The practical question is not “technology or no technology,” but which failure modes we accept and which we regulate: emissions and safety for vehicles, privacy and platform incentives for networks, evaluation and deployment limits for AI. Trade-offs exist. A return to life without these tools does not resolve them; it mostly reintroduces older, harsher ones.

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

No. Effectiveness at matching ads to preferences is not, by itself, a good reason to ban the technology. A blanket ban would also wipe out a lot of ordinary, useful personalization and would be nearly impossible to draw cleanly. The real problem is not that an ad system “knows” someone well. It is when that knowledge is used without meaningful consent, in ways people cannot see or refuse, or to exploit vulnerability rather than inform a choice. Highly tuned recommendation systems can nudge spending, attention, and beliefs, especially when combined with opaque profiling, emotional targeting, or design meant to keep people engaged. Those risks justify limits. They do not justify treating every personalized ad model as illegitimate. Better responses than a ban: - Require clear disclosure and easy, durable opt-outs from profiling and personalized ads. - Restrict sensitive inferences and targeting (health, finances, minors, political or similarly high-stakes categories). - Ban manipulative practices: exploiting known distress, dark patterns, and ads designed to override deliberation rather than present an offer. - Give people access to the main categories of data and inferences used, and a way to correct or delete them. - Hold platforms accountable for discriminatory or deceptive outcomes, not merely for being accurate. Familiarity becomes unacceptable when the user cannot understand, contest, or leave it. Regulation should target that loss of control and the concrete harms, not the mere fact that an ad system is good at its job.
