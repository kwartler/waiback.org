Technology have become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

In general, humans would not be better off abandoning vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications, or AI altogether. These technologies create real problems, but they also support health, education, communication, and economic opportunity. The better question is how to use them without allowing them to undermine human well-being. **Vehicles** make it possible to transport food, reach hospitals, and connect distant communities. However, they also cause accidents, pollution, and congestion. Rather than eliminating vehicles, societies could invest in reliable public transport, safer roads, and walkable neighborhoods. **The Internet and cellular telecommunications** help people learn, maintain relationships, work remotely, and seek help during emergencies. They are especially valuable for people who live far from essential services. At the same time, constant connectivity can encourage distraction, spread misinformation, and threaten privacy. Stronger privacy protections, digital literacy, and healthy boundaries can reduce these harms without sacrificing the benefits. **AI** can assist with research, improve accessibility, and reduce repetitive work. However, its benefits are not automatic: it can produce errors, reinforce unfairness, displace workers, and concentrate power. Its use should therefore depend on the situation. A tool that helps someone translate a document poses different risks from a system influencing medical treatment or employment decisions. Higher-stakes uses need careful testing, accountability, and meaningful human oversight; some uses may deserve restrictions or rejection. Living with less technology can still be a worthwhile personal choice. Time offline, face-to-face relationships, physical activity, and practical skills can improve quality of life. Communities should also retain alternatives for people who cannot—or choose not to—use digital services. Ultimately, **a better life does not require either rejecting technology or accepting every innovation**. It requires choosing technologies according to human needs, sharing their benefits fairly, and preserving the freedom to limit their role. Technology should serve people, not become something people must organize their entire lives around.

The prospect of admission to a better school is a reason to offer support—not a reason to ignore how that support is provided. And a more selective school doesn’t guarantee better long-term prospects; fit, cost, and what the student does there also matter. **The key distinction is between coaching the applicant and substituting for the applicant.** An admissions essay typically represents the student’s experiences, judgment, voice, and ability to communicate. Assistance becomes misleading when it supplies what the application is supposed to reveal about the student. A useful boundary is: | Usually appropriate, if application rules allow it | Likely to cross into cheating or misrepresentation | |---|---| | Asking questions that help the student identify meaningful experiences | Inventing experiences, accomplishments, or personal insights | | Pointing out confusing passages or unsupported claims | Creating the central message and reflection for the student | | Teaching revision techniques and explaining grammar corrections | Producing a polished replacement essay for submission | | Suggesting limited edits while preserving the student’s voice | Making the student sound like a substantially different writer | | Giving feedback on a student’s own revision | Helping conceal assistance that must be disclosed | **“The student approved every sentence” isn’t enough.** Someone can approve a ghostwritten essay without having authored it. The distinction is whether AI helps the application reveal the student more clearly—or creates a more impressive applicant on the page than the work genuinely represents.
