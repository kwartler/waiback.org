Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Not entirely. Humans would likely be worse off without all of these technologies—but could benefit from being less dependent on them. The key distinction is between **using technology to support human well-being and organizing life so that people cannot function without it**. Each technology brings different trade-offs: - **Vehicles** enable emergency care, food distribution, and access to work and family. Yet car dependence contributes to pollution, road deaths, and sedentary lifestyles. Walkable communities and reliable public transport are generally a better goal than eliminating vehicles. - **The Internet** expands access to knowledge, services, and human connection. It also enables surveillance, misinformation, scams, and constant distraction. People benefit from access without being forced to conduct every part of life online. - **Cellular telecommunications** make it easier to get help and stay connected, especially while traveling or in remote areas with coverage. The downside is the expectation of constant availability, along with privacy risks and compulsive use. - **AI** can support research, accessibility, and useful automation. But it can also produce misleading information, amplify bias, undermine privacy, and disrupt employment. Its benefits depend on where it is used, how reliable it is, and whether humans retain meaningful oversight. A simpler, less connected life can offer real advantages: more attention, physical activity, privacy, and face-to-face interaction. But those benefits are not equally available to everyone. Someone who relies on assistive technology, remote medical care, or communication across long distances may lose far more than they gain by disconnecting. So the better question may be: **Which technologies genuinely improve our lives, and which forms of dependence should we reduce?** A good society would preserve their benefits while making room for offline services, human judgment, strong communities, and the ability to disconnect.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

AI should help a student improve a college application essay, but generally should not rewrite it into something the student could not honestly call their own. The prospect of admission to a better school is a reason to offer support—not a reason to ignore how that support is provided. And a more selective school doesn’t guarantee better long-term prospects; fit, cost, and what the student does there also matter. **The key distinction is between coaching the applicant and substituting for the applicant.** An admissions essay typically represents the student’s experiences, judgment, voice, and ability to communicate. Assistance becomes misleading when it supplies what the application is supposed to reveal about the student. A useful boundary is: | Usually appropriate, if application rules allow it | Likely to cross into cheating or misrepresentation | |---|---| | Asking questions that help the student identify meaningful experiences | Inventing experiences, accomplishments, or personal insights | | Pointing out confusing passages or unsupported claims | Creating the central message and reflection for the student | | Teaching revision techniques and explaining grammar corrections | Producing a polished replacement essay for submission | | Suggesting limited edits while preserving the student’s voice | Making the student sound like a substantially different writer | | Giving feedback on a student’s own revision | Helping conceal assistance that must be disclosed | **“The student approved every sentence” isn’t enough.** Someone can approve a ghostwritten essay without having authored it. Conversely, not every corrected sentence makes an essay dishonest: ordinary proofreading and feedback can leave authorship intact. I’d use three tests: 1. **Rules:** Does the college or application platform permit this kind of assistance? Follow its actual policy, including any disclosure requirement. 2. **Authorship:** Did the student supply the experiences, interpretation, central ideas, and substantive writing—not merely a few facts for AI to turn into an essay? 3. **Transparency:** Could the student accurately describe the help they received without misleading the admissions reader? If I were helping, I’d favor questions, critique, and explanations, then ask the student to do the rewriting. For example: “This paragraph says the experience changed you, but doesn’t show how. What did you do differently afterward?” That develops the student’s expression rather than replacing it. There’s a real fairness argument for AI support: wealthier applicants already receive extensive human coaching. Affordable feedback can broaden access. But that supports making **coaching** more accessible, not treating undisclosed ghostwriting as acceptable. So my answer is **yes to helping the child write a stronger essay; no to quietly becoming its author**. The distinction is whether AI helps the application reveal the student more clearly—or creates a more impressive applicant on the page than the work genuinely represents.
