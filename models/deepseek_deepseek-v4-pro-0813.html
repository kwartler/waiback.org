Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

No, it would not be better for humans to live without these technologies altogether—but that does not mean they are harmless or that we should use them uncritically. The question is usually framed as a choice between “technology” and “a more natural, human life.” But removing vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications, or AI would not return us to a simpler paradise. It would more likely produce a poorer, more isolated, and more vulnerable world. Consider what each technology actually does. **Vehicles** Without vehicles—cars, trucks, trains, ships, planes—modern food distribution would collapse. Cities depend on daily deliveries of food, medicine, and fuel. Emergency services like ambulances and fire trucks would be impossible. Rural areas would become even more isolated. Economic activity, healthcare access, and disaster response all rely on motorized transport. The costs of vehicles are real: accidents, pollution, urban sprawl, and dependence on fossil fuels. But the solution is better vehicles, cleaner energy, safer design, and better public transit—not no vehicles. **The Internet** The Internet is not just entertainment and social media. It is also the infrastructure of modern knowledge, science, education, banking, government, and global coordination. Medical research, weather forecasting, supply chains, and human rights documentation all depend on it. Without the Internet, access to information would shrink drastically, and many people—especially in remote or marginalized communities—would lose access to education, markets, and public services. The Internet has serious problems: misinformation, addiction, privacy violations, and concentration of power. But those are problems of governance and design, not reasons to abolish the network. **Cellular telecommunications** Mobile phones and cellular networks have extended communication to billions of people who never had landlines. They enable emergency calls, mobile banking, remote health advice, disaster warnings, and economic participation in developing countries. Without cellular telecommunications, many communities would lose their only reliable connection to the wider world. The downsides—constant distraction, social pressure, mental health effects—are real, but they can be addressed through better habits, regulation, and design. **AI** AI is the newest and most contested of these technologies. It is already embedded in medical diagnosis, fraud detection, translation, navigation, accessibility tools, logistics, and scientific research. Removing AI would not simply mean losing chatbots; it would mean losing pattern-recognition systems that help read X-rays, predict weather, optimize food distribution, and assist people with disabilities. At the same time, AI raises serious concerns: job displacement, bias, surveillance, concentration of corporate power, and possible long-term risks. Of the four, AI is the one we could most plausibly live without in the short term, because human alternatives still exist for many tasks. But a complete ban would still cause significant harm, especially in medicine, science, and accessibility. So the honest answer is: **none of these technologies should be rejected wholesale.** They are not neutral tools—they shape society, create winners and losers, and can be misused. But their absence would not automatically make life better. It would more likely make life shorter, harder, and more unequal. The better question is not “Should we live without them?” but “How should we live with them?” That means: - Designing vehicles and cities for safety and sustainability, not just speed and profit. - Governing the Internet to protect privacy, truth, and democratic participation. - Using mobile technology to connect people without turning attention into a commodity. - Developing AI with transparency, accountability, and human oversight. Technology has become part of everyday life because it solves real problems. The goal is not to escape it, but to make it serve human flourishing rather than undermine it.
