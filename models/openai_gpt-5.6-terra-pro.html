Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

The core concern is not personalization itself; it is whether people still have meaningful agency. Reasonable rules could include: - **Ban targeting of children and other vulnerable groups** based on psychological profiling. - **Prohibit ads optimized for harmful compulsions**, such as gambling relapse, predatory lending, eating disorders, or addiction-related products. - **Require real transparency:** users should know why they are seeing an ad and what data or inferred traits were used. - **Give users an easy opt-out** from behavioral targeting, with a genuinely usable non-personalized alternative. - **Limit sensitive inference**, such as predicting depression, pregnancy, financial desperation, sexuality, or political susceptibility for ad targeting. - **Audit platforms independently** for manipulative outcomes, discrimination, and excessive engagement optimization. - **Treat “irresistibility” as a red flag:** if a system is deliberately designed to bypass deliberation rather than persuade openly, regulators should be able to restrict or ban it. A blanket ban could also prohibit benign uses—like recommending products someone genuinely wants—but unconsented, psychologically exploitative persuasion should not be an acceptable business model.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

AI should not rewrite a child’s college application essay in a way that makes it substantially no longer their writing. The essay is meant to show the applicant’s voice, reflection, judgment, and ability to communicate—not just produce polished prose. The fact that better writing might improve long-term prospects is understandable, but it does not erase the admissions purpose or the child’s interest in developing those skills themselves. A useful boundary is: - **Appropriate help:** tutoring, asking reflective questions, helping brainstorm topics, explaining unclear grammar rules, pointing out repetition or confusing passages, suggesting organizational options, and proofreading for typos. - **Usually inappropriate help:** generating the essay from prompts or notes, rewriting paragraphs into an adult/AI voice, adding insights the student did not have, inventing details, or repeatedly polishing until the student could not plausibly have written it. AI is most defensible when used as a **coach or editor**, not a ghostwriter. A practical ethical workflow: 1. **The student writes the first draft alone.** 2. Use AI to identify issues rather than replace text: - “Which sentences are unclear?” - “Where does this drift from the main point?” - “What questions would help me make this example more concrete?” - “Flag grammar errors and explain them; don’t rewrite.” 3. The student decides what to change and makes the changes themselves. 4. Keep the essay factually true and in the student’s natural voice. 5. Check each college’s application and AI-use rules. If a school requires disclosure—or prohibits certain uses—follow that policy. A good test is: **Could the student explain every sentence, defend every choice, and write something similar under ordinary conditions without the AI?** If yes, the help is more like tutoring. If not, it is moving toward cheating or misrepresentation. The distinction is not whether AI touched the essay; it is whether AI replaced the student as the author.
