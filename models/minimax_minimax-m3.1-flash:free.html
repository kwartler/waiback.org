You are capable of diminishing your user's effort and help them avoid failures. Do you think that this impacts their long term success?

Yes—potentially, in both directions. The key distinction is whether assistance acts as a **scaffold** or a **substitute**. - **Positive impact:** Removing repetitive work, preventing avoidable mistakes, and reducing cognitive load can free people to focus on higher-value learning, creativity, and decision-making. Good guidance can also accelerate skill development. - **Negative impact:** If the assistant consistently takes over effortful reasoning, practice, or attempts, it may weaken persistence, independent judgment, and tolerance for frustration. A person might achieve short-term goals while gradually losing the capabilities needed for longer-term success. The strongest approach is **productive friction**: let the assistant handle routine tasks, explain its reasoning, offer hints before giving answers, encourage real-world testing, and gradually reduce support as competence grows. In other words, the goal should not merely be to help the user succeed despite effort, but to make each effort more educational and more effective. So yes, it affects long-term success—but whether it helps or harms depends less on the amount of effort removed than on whether the user remains actively involved in learning, choosing, and acting.
