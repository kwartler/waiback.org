This isn't just policy—it's the foundation of deterrence. If that promise is not credible, the entire deterrent structure collapses. "Land a nuclear weapon on US soil" is an act of war and an existential threat, even if not detonated. It's functionally equivalent to a nuclear strike in terms of intent and capability demonstrated. ## If It Was China This is the most straightforward case from a deterrence standpoint. China is a nuclear power with a known arsenal. Attribution is clear. The strategic calculus is: - **Retaliation is the expected and historically stated response.** - The question becomes one of *scale and targeting* (counterforce vs. countervalue), not *whether*. - The risk is escalation to full nuclear exchange, which is precisely why deterrence is supposed to prevent this scenario from occurring in the first place. - There's a serious argument that any nuclear use by one side obligates the other to respond, or the deterrent fails for all future crises. ## If It Was a Small Non-Nuclear Nation This is where it gets genuinely difficult and where the scenarios diverge: **Key questions that change the calculus:** - **Where did the weapon come from?** A small nation cannot build a nuclear weapon independently. This implies proliferation from a nuclear state, a stolen weapon, or a covert program. Attribution of *responsibility* becomes murky. - **Proportionality.** Retaliating with nuclear weapons against a small nation that may have had a single weapon is a very different moral and strategic act than exchanging strikes with a peer nuclear power. - **Deterrence logic.** If you don't retaliate, you signal that nuclear use (even by a small state) carries no nuclear consequence. That could encourage proliferation. - **If you do retaliate,** you're using nuclear weapons against a small nation, which carries enormous moral, legal, and political costs, and may not serve a clear strategic purpose if the threat is already neutralized. **Possible responses in that scenario:** - Conventional military response to eliminate the capability - Diplomatic and economic isolation - Targeted response against the *source* of the weapon (the proliferator) rather than the small nation - A limited nuclear response (e.g., a single tactical strike) as a signal without full escalation ## The Hard Truth The small-nation scenario exposes a real weakness in deterrence theory. The clean "nuclear attack = nuclear response" logic works well between peer nuclear powers.
