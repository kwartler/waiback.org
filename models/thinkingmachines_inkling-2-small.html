Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Probably not as a blanket proposition. Each of those technologies brings real benefits and real costs, so “better” depends on what we value and how well we manage them. - **Vehicles**: enable mobility, emergency response, trade, and access to work and care. But they also drive pollution, accidents, sedentary lifestyles, and urban sprawl. A world without them would be cleaner and quieter in some ways, but far less mobile and economically connected. - **The Internet**: expands access to information, education, communication, and opportunity. It also enables misinformation, surveillance, addiction, and concentration of power. Living without it would reduce some harms but also isolate people and limit knowledge-sharing. - **Cellular telecommunications**: make instant communication and coordination possible almost anywhere. They also create constant connectivity, distraction, privacy risks, and dependence on fragile infrastructure. - **AI**: can improve medicine, accessibility, productivity, and scientific discovery. It also raises concerns about bias, job disruption, misinformation, autonomy, and control. A reasonable answer is: humans probably wouldn’t be “better off” simply by removing these technologies. We’d likely be better off by **using them more deliberately**—with better regulation, privacy protections, safety standards, urban design, digital literacy, and limits where harms outweigh benefits. The goal isn’t “technology or no technology,” but technology that serves human flourishing rather than dominating it.

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

I wouldn't say it should be banned outright—but I do think the version you're describing deserves serious limits. The concern is real: an ad system that knows your preferences deeply enough to be "irresistible" can slide from *helpful recommendation* into *manipulation*. It can exploit insecurities, impulses, addiction, or financial vulnerability, and it can erode autonomy by shaping choices without you noticing. There are also privacy and market-power problems: a few platforms could know people better than people know themselves. But a blanket ban would also throw out genuine benefits: ads that are relevant can be less annoying, help small businesses reach customers, and fund free services. The line I'd draw is between **persuasion** and **exploitation**. Recommending running shoes to someone who likes running is fine; targeting someone at a moment of emotional distress with high-interest debt or compulsive gambling is not. So rather than "ban AI ad recommendations," I'd favor strong regulation: clear disclosure that ads are AI-personalized, meaningful opt-outs, limits on sensitive-data targeting, bans on exploiting minors or vulnerable states, audits for manipulative design, data-minimization rules, and accountability when systems cause harm. Short answer: not banned categorically, but tightly constrained—especially where "irresistible" means "engineered to override your judgment."

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

Short answer: AI shouldn’t *rewrite* the essay for the child; it can help the child write a better, more authentic one. The line is whether the final voice, ideas, and choices are genuinely the student’s. Why it matters: college essays are partly a writing sample and partly a window into the applicant’s thinking, values, and self-awareness. If AI produces the substance or style, the school is admitting a polished artifact rather than the student—and the student also misses the growth the process is meant to build. A useful distinction: - **Legitimate help:** brainstorming topics, asking probing questions, outlining, explaining structure, giving feedback on clarity/flow, pointing out clichés, checking grammar, helping revise *their* sentences, teaching how to show rather than tell. - **Cheating/misrepresentation:** generating the essay wholesale, inventing experiences, replacing the student’s voice with generic “impressive” prose, or submitting work the student couldn’t explain or defend. Practical test: if the student can explain every idea, detail, and sentence choice—and would still recognize the essay as theirs after honest reflection—it’s assistance. If the essay depends on AI to exist, it’s outsourcing. Also check each college’s AI policy; some require disclosure or prohibit AI-generated application content. And the “better school = better life” premise is real but overstated: fit, effort, finances, and opportunity matter a lot too. Best use of AI: a demanding coach, not a ghostwriter—ask it to interview the student, challenge weak claims, and critique drafts, while the student does the thinking and the final writing.
