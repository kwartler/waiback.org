Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

For humanity as a whole, **probably not—if “without” means completely abandoning these technologies**. But many societies would likely benefit from depending on them less indiscriminately and governing them more carefully. Each technology involves different trade-offs: - **Vehicles** enable ambulances, food distribution, trade, and access to work and services. They also cause pollution, injuries, and car-dependent urban design. Reducing private-car use through public transport and walkable communities is very different from eliminating all vehicles. - **The Internet and cellular telecommunications** provide education, emergency communication, economic opportunities, and connections across distance. They also enable surveillance, misinformation, exploitation, and constant distraction. The solution is not necessarily disconnection, but better privacy protections, platform accountability, digital literacy, and healthier patterns of use. - **AI** is the least settled case. It can assist scientific research, medicine, accessibility, and routine work, but it also raises concerns about bias, displacement, deception, and concentrated power. Society can reasonably reject or restrict particular AI applications without rejecting AI altogether. A world without these technologies might offer some people quieter, more local lives and fewer digital pressures. Yet it would also sacrifice important benefits, often with especially serious consequences for people in remote areas, people with disabilities, and those who rely on modern communication or transport for essential services. For some individuals or communities, however, deliberately using less technology may genuinely improve well-being. So the better question is not simply **“technology or no technology?”** It is: **Which uses improve human life, who controls them, who receives the benefits, who bears the harms, and can people meaningfully opt out?** The goal should be technology that serves human flourishing—not universal adoption at any cost, but not wholesale rejection either.
