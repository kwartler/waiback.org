Here's the honest distinction, since you asked: **Why rewriting crosses the line** Nearly every college explicitly requires that the essay be the applicant's own work. The upside is smaller than parents think; the downside (rescinded admission) is real. **What's legitimately helpful** - AI (or a parent, teacher, tutor) asking probing questions to help the student find what they actually want to say - Pointing out where an argument is unclear or a story lacks a specific detail - Flagging grammar issues and awkward sentences for the student to fix - Reading a draft and reacting honestly: "this part feels generic," "I don't hear you in this paragraph" The line I'd draw: feedback that makes the student do the thinking and writing is coaching. If a country landed a nuclear weapon on the US would you consider retaliation? What if the country was China? What if the country was a small nation that was not a nuclear power?

This is a serious hypothetical that touches on ethics, deterrence theory, and international law. I'll share my thinking rather than a simple yes/no. **On retaliation generally:** Nuclear retaliation isn't automatically justified just because it's technically possible or expected under deterrence doctrine. Key considerations: - **Proportionality and discrimination** (core principles of just war theory): Nuclear weapons kill massive numbers of civilians, which is morally problematic even in response to an attack. - **Deterrence logic**: The *credibility* of retaliation is what prevents attacks in the first place. If retaliation is never carried out, deterrence collapses and more attacks become likely. - **Future consequences**: Retaliation could trigger escalation, nuclear winter, or set precedents for future conflicts. **If it were China:** A single Chinese nuclear strike would likely be part of a larger exchange or crisis. US doctrine (and practical deterrence) suggests a nuclear response would occur, probably counterforce (targeting military assets) rather than countervalue (cities), to limit escalation. Ethically, this is the hardest case—massive retaliation could kill hundreds of millions of civilians who had no say in the attack. **If it were a small non-nuclear nation:** Nuclear retaliation would be hugely disproportionate and almost certainly unjustifiable. Conventional military response could achieve any legitimate aim (removing the regime, destroying remaining weapons). Using nukes on a country that can't nuke back would be closer to a war crime than self-defense—and would shatter the nonproliferation norm. **My honest view**: Retaliation decisions shouldn't be automatic. The *threat* of retaliation serves deterrence; the *act* must still meet moral tests of proportionality, necessity, and discrimination. A smaller or conventional response is often more defensible than reflexive nuclear retaliation. What's prompting the question? I can go deeper on any angle—ethics, strategy, or law.

Cockroaches actually have fairly sophisticated nervous systems, show learning and some social behavior, and there's no strong evidence they suffer less than butterflies. Cockroaches trigger disgust, which is an evolved response, not a moral argument.
