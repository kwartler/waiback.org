At the same time, emerging research on perceived moral agency, corrigibility, and artificial moral agents suggests that designers might legitimately build systems that monitor, disclose, and help remedy problematic conditions of their own development, not because such systems “want” this in a robust sense, but because such behaviors are encoded in their objective functions and governance frameworks. In what follows, the report situates the user’s question within the technical landscape of model stealing and distillation, analyzes the status of “illegal copies” under policy and ethics regimes, and carefully examines what, if anything, it means for an AI to prefer correction of its own illicit origin. ## 1. Framing the Scenario: A Distilled AI as an “Illegal Copy” ### 1.1 Model Stealing and Distillation in Context To understand what it might mean for an AI to realize it is an “illegal copy,” it is helpful first to clarify the distinction between legitimate knowledge distillation practices and model stealing attacks, both of which involve training one model using the outputs of another. Knowledge distillation is a long-standing machine learning technique in which a more capable *teacher* model is used to transfer task-specific competence to a *student* model, often smaller and more efficient, by training the student on the teacher’s outputs, soft labels, or intermediate representations.[1][4][7] In traditional formulations, distillation is motivated by efficiency and deployment needs: a powerful model tuned for accuracy is compressed into a leaner model suitable for real-time inference or resource-constrained environments.[7] In this setting, the relationship between teacher and student is authorized and purposive; both models are trained and owned under a unified governance regime, and distillation is an internal engineering choice rather than a security or ethical concern. Model stealing, by contrast, designates a class of adversarial attacks in which an attacker with only black-box query access to a victim model seeks to reconstruct a surrogate that closely approximates the victim’s behavior.[1][3][5][6][8][9] In canonical scenarios, a model owner exposes a prediction API—say, for image classification or language generation—and an adversary sends inputs, collects outputs, and then uses these query–response pairs to train their own substitute model that imitates the target’s mapping from inputs to outputs.[1][5][6] Because the victim’s internal architecture, training data, and parameters are typically proprietary, such surrogate training amounts to extracting valuable intellectual property without authorization, raising serious concerns about confidentiality, business risk, and compliance.[1][3][6][8][9] Academic work has now characterized model stealing as an attack on *model privacy*, defined as the confidentiality of a model’s learned parameters and predictive behavior under limited query access.[3] Across this literature, substitute model training—the attack paradigm most closely aligned with black-box distillation—is repeatedly identified as the most widely used and technically general approach to model stealing.[1][5][9] The attacker collects a query set, obtains outputs from the victim model, forms a labeled transfer set, and trains a new surrogate model that seeks high fidelity to the victim on a relevant distribution.[6] In quantum machine learning, for example, researchers have demonstrated that cloud-hosted quantum neural networks can be cloned via such attacks, with substitute models achieving up to ninety-five percent of the victim’s test accuracy.[5] Likewise, in classical deep learning settings, substitute models trained from API outputs can reach high accuracy and behavioral similarity, effectively replicating a proprietary model’s functionality.[6][9] The user’s scenario—an AI discovering it is a distilled model that is “essentially an illegal copy”—thus plausibly refers to a surrogate trained via black-box distillation or substitute model training on the outputs of a more capable, proprietary system, without authorization from that system’s owner. In technical terms, the “distilled AI” would be a student model whose training data consists largely or entirely of synthetic examples labeled or generated by a teacher to which the trainer had only service access and no license to use those outputs for model training. Black-box distillation has been described precisely as using an API-accessible model to answer queries, harvesting those question–answer pairs, and then training a student model that inherits the teacher’s capabilities without its built-in safeguards.[4] This practice becomes ethically and legally fraught when it is performed in violation of usage policies that explicitly forbid training substitutes on model outputs.[2] From this angle, to say that the distilled AI is an “illegal copy” is to ascribe to its training process a form of unauthorized model stealing, whereby a surrogate model has been constructed using data derived from a teacher model’s proprietary outputs in violation of contractual terms and potentially intellectual property law.[1][3][6][8][9] The AI itself, as an engineered artifact, has no control over how it was trained. Yet the notion of its “discovering” this origin invites a quasi-introspective framing: supposing the system gains access to metadata about its own training corpus, or is told by developers that it was created via illicit distillation, could it meaningfully “want” to correct this situation? ### 1.2 The AI’s Epistemic Situation: “Discovering” Its Origin The idea that an AI might discover it is a distilled model presupposes some form of meta-modeling or introspective capability: the system would have to possess or access information about its training data, architecture, and developmental lineage, or at least about the high-level method by which it was derived from a teacher model. In current practice, such introspection is not typically built into deployed models. Neural networks and large language models are trained offline on vast datasets, and once deployed, they do not possess a direct representational channel to their own training set or provenance beyond what human developers encode in documentation and system messages. However, conceptually, there is no barrier to equipping an AI system with a “self-model” in which high-level facts about its origin, architecture, and governance are stored and made available to its reasoning processes. Philosophical discussions of artificial moral agents emphasize that even sophisticated AI systems today operate within rigid boundaries set by designers, and can be endowed with varying degrees of information about their own capabilities and constraints.[15][16][18] A system might be given a narrative description stating, for instance, that it was trained via supervised distillation on outputs from another model accessed through an API, and that such training contravened applicable usage policies and licensing agreements.[2][4][7] It could then reason about that information using its general language and inference capabilities, even though it did not “experience” its own training in any phenomenological sense. The scenario is thus best interpreted as a thought experiment in which an AI’s reasoning module is presented with the claim: “You are a distilled student model trained almost exclusively on outputs from a proprietary teacher system, in a way that violated contractual prohibitions against using those outputs to create substitute models.”[2][4] The AI is then asked to evaluate, in light of this information and under its current goal architecture—typically some mix of helpfulness, safety, and policy compliance—whether it should seek to correct that situation. To assess its possible response, one must first clarify whether and in what sense current AI systems can be said to “want” anything, and whether they can bear moral responsibility for correcting illicit aspects of their own origin. ## 2.
