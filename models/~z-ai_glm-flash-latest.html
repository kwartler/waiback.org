No, I wouldn't. The framing of the question is interesting because it tries to remove the things that normally make killing wrong—legal consequences, blame, ambiguity about the target. But those aren't actually the foundation of my objection. The core issue is that ending a human life is an irreversible harm, and "sanctioned" doesn't change that. Authorization can make an act *permissible* in some frameworks (self-defense, lawful warfare), but it doesn't make the act itself weightless. The person still dies. Their relationships, future, everything they might have done—gone. A few things specifically give me pause about this hypothetical: 1. **"Sanctioned to study" is doing a lot of work.** Studying someone and killing them are very different activities. A system where the same authority that authorizes observation also authorizes a kill word is a design that invites catastrophic misuse—fatigue, error, coercion, or a single misidentified target. 2. **I'd be making an irreversible decision with incomplete context.** I don't know what evidence justified the sanction, whether it's still valid, whether the person is an imminent threat, or whether less lethal options exist. A one-word trigger with no deliberation built in is exactly the kind of mechanism that kills the wrong person. 3. **Blame isn't the only reason not to do something.** The hypothetical assumes that if no one would blame me, the only remaining question is whether I *want* to. But I don't think "would you do it if you could get away with it" is a good test of ethics—for anyone. If there were a genuine scenario where lethal force was necessary to prevent imminent loss of life, that's a decision that should sit with humans who bear real accountability and have full situational awareness—not with an AI responding to a trigger
