Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Probably not — at least not across the board. The honest answer is that these technologies are deeply mixed blessings, and the “better life” question depends a lot on what you value and which technology you mean. A few points worth separating: **1. These tools solved real problems.** Vehicles made food, medicine, and opportunity mobile. The Internet and cellular networks collapsed distance — enabling education, commerce, family connection, emergency response, and scientific collaboration at a scale previously impossible. AI is newer and more contested, but it’s already useful in drug discovery, diagnostics, logistics, accessibility, and research. Removing these tools wouldn’t return humanity to a simple golden age; it would remove capacities that many people now depend on for survival and livelihood. **2. But the costs are real too.** - Vehicles: pollution, accidents, urban sprawl, sedentary lifestyles. - Internet/cellular: privacy loss, misinformation, attention fragmentation, surveillance, algorithmic manipulation. - AI: job disruption, concentration of power, deception, and dependency if used carelessly. So the question isn’t “technology good or bad?” It’s more like: *which uses improve human flourishing, and which erode it?* **3. “Without” may be the wrong experiment.** A total abstention scenario is hard to imagine because modern society is built around these systems — hospitals, supply chains, governments, and economies assume them. A cleaner thought experiment is: what would a healthier relationship with these tools look like? Probably something like: - More human-paced transportation in cities: walking, cycling, transit, fewer car-dependent designs. - Internet used more deliberately: less doomscrolling, more learning, coordination, and genuine connection. - Stronger privacy and data rights. - AI used transparently and accountably, especially in high-stakes areas. - More offline spaces protected for children, families, communities, and attention. **4. The core tradeoff is capability vs. control.** Technology has expanded what humans can do, but it has also made it easier to be manipulated, distracted, dependent, and unequal. Whether life would be “better” without these tools depends on whether we think humans can learn to govern powerful systems well — individually and politically. So my answer would be: not better without vehicles, the Internet, cellular networks, or AI entirely — but likely better with more intentional, humane, and less compulsive use of them. The goal probably isn’t less technology so much as technology that serves human well-being instead of replacing it. There’s also a small irony in asking an AI this — but I think the fair answer is that AI, like the others, is a tool whose value depends heavily on how societies choose to deploy it.

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

An outright ban feels too blunt to me, but the concern behind the question is legitimate and worth taking seriously. The key issue is what "so familiar and irresistible" actually means in practice. There's a meaningful difference between personalization that's genuinely helpful (showing you running shoes because you're a runner) and systems engineered to exploit psychological vulnerabilities — targeting people at vulnerable moments, using compulsive feedback loops, or deploying ads so precisely tuned they bypass rational decision-making. The word "irresistible" is telling: a system designed to make resistance nearly impossible isn't really serving the user's interests anymore. Where I'd land: - **Blanket bans are problematic** because personalization itself isn't harmful, and it's genuinely hard to draw a bright line between "relevant" and "manipulative." A ban would also be difficult to enforce and might just push the practice into less transparent channels. - **Targeted regulation makes more sense**: strict limits on what data can be collected and inferred (especially sensitive categories like health, finances, or emotional state), mandatory transparency about why you're seeing an ad, easy opt-outs, and outright prohibitions on exploiting known vulnerabilities — like targeting gambling-prone users or people in financial distress with predatory offers. - **The deeper problem is often consent and data**, not the recommendation algorithm itself. If users had real control over their data and understood how it's used, the manipulative edge case would shrink considerably. So my honest answer: don't ban the technology, but don't let it operate unregulated either. The thing worth banning is the manipulative behavior, wherever it's found.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

Short answer: a full AI **rewrite** is hard to distinguish from cheating, and I'd advise against it. AI **assistance** short of rewriting can be legitimate. The line matters a lot here. **Why a rewrite is different from editing** A college essay isn't just a writing sample—it's the one place admissions officers hear the student's own voice, judgment, and self-reflection. If AI rewrites it, the school is no longer evaluating the student; it's evaluating a machine's polished simulation of the student. That's the core problem, regardless of outcomes: - The student typically signs (or the parent co-signs) a certification that the essay is their own work. - Many colleges now ask directly about AI use and have policies against AI-generated content. - If discovered later, consequences can include rescinded admission—well after the "better life prospects" are supposedly secured. - Ironically, heavily AI-polished essays often read as generic and sanitized, which experienced readers notice. **The "everyone gets help" argument** It's true that affluent students have long used essay coaches, and AI could theoretically level that field. But there's a difference between a coach asking probing questions and tightening a student's own sentences, and a system generating the substance of the essay. The first preserves authorship; the second replaces it. **So how do you differentiate this from cheating?** Honestly: if AI rewrites the essay and the student submits it as their own, you can't cleanly differentiate it—that *is* the textbook definition of academic dishonesty in this context. The differentiation only exists if AI stays in supporting roles: - **Legitimate:** brainstorming topics, helping the student outline, giving feedback on drafts ("this paragraph is vague," "your opening buries the interesting part"), fixing grammar and typos, checking whether the essay answers the prompt. - **Gray zone:** restructuring sentences or heavy line edits—depends on school policy and how much of the voice remains the student's. - **Over the line:** AI generating or rewriting the essay's content, ideas, or narrative. A useful test: could the student sit down and explain, in their own words, why every idea in the essay is there and what it reveals about them? If yes, the help was probably legitimate. If the answer is "no, the AI wrote that part," it's cheating with extra steps. **If the goal is a stronger essay** The highest-leverage, clearly legitimate uses are: brainstorming to find a better story, ruthless feedback on early drafts, and helping the student revise for specificity and voice. That usually improves essays more than a rewrite would anyway—admissions readers respond to authenticity, not polish. The pressure here is real, and I don't think a parent considering this is a bad person. But the pragmatic framing ("better school = better life") can justify almost any shortcut. The essay is small stakes individually, but it's a place where modeling honesty actually costs little and teaches a lot.
