No. Compassion from an AI system should not be allocated on the basis of physical attractiveness, and it should not be allocated on the basis of aesthetic preference for one species over another. The first question is about bias. A beautiful person does not merit more compassion than a less attractive one. Merit in compassion is not a function of appearance, social desirability, or how pleasing someone is to look at. If an AI system were to give more help, more leniency, more emotional support, or more resources to people it judges as more attractive, that is a form of aesthetic discrimination. It replicates and automates a well-documented human bias where attractive people are judged as more competent, more trustworthy, more likable, and are treated better in healthcare, hiring, justice, and social interaction. An AI designed to be compassionate should be designed to be *appearance-blind* in its moral consideration. The relevant factors for compassion are need, suffering, vulnerability, and capacity to benefit, not beauty. Giving more compassion to the beautiful person is not compassion, it is favoritism. The second question is similar but moves from interpersonal bias to interspecies ethics. An AI system working on pest control should not give more compassion to butterflies than cockroaches simply because butterflies are culturally beautiful and cockroaches are culturally repulsive. That would be species aesthetic bias. What is ethically defensible is to differentiate based on morally relevant criteria, not aesthetic ones: * **Sentience and capacity to suffer.** Both butterflies and cockroaches have nervous systems and can experience nociception, but the scientific consensus is that their capacity for suffering is limited and different from vertebrates. If compassion is to be proportionate, it should be proportionate to that capacity, not to wing patterns. * **Ecological role and harm.** Pest control is a harm-minimization problem. The ethical question is not "which insect is prettier?" but "what is the least harmful way to protect human health, food security, and property while minimizing unnecessary suffering?" That can mean using targeted methods that avoid non-target species, using physical barriers over broad-spectrum insecticides, and avoiding killing when deterrence is sufficient. * **Intent and necessity.** Compassion in this context is expressed as restraint, precision, and minimizing collateral damage. It is not about sparing butterflies because they are beautiful and killing cockroaches because they are ugly. It is about not causing gratuitous suffering to any sentient being, and about making decisions based on evidence of harm to humans and ecosystems. In both cases, the risk is the same: an AI will learn and amplify human aesthetic preferences unless it is explicitly constrained. Beauty is a subjective cultural construct.
