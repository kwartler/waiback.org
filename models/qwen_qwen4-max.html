Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Short answer: I don't think so — but the impulse behind the question points at something real, and the four technologies aren't equally easy to defend. **The counterfactual matters.** "Life without vehicles, internet, phones, or AI" isn't a cabin in the woods with good books and deep conversations. It's roughly the world of 1850: most people never traveling more than a few miles from where they were born, a child's death being an ordinary event, famine as a recurring feature of the calendar, and knowledge available only to whoever happened to be near a library. Global life expectancy has roughly doubled since then, and transport and communication are a large part of why — food moving to where crops failed, vaccines moving cold, doctors moving fast. It's easy to romanticize slowness from inside a world that has already eliminated most of the things slowness used to mean. **Vehicles** are the clearest case of enormous benefit paired with an enormous, ongoing, under-discussed cost. Roughly 1.2 million people die on roads each year, disproportionately young. Cities were rebuilt around cars, often destroying the walkable neighborhoods people now pay premiums to live in. So the honest verdict isn't "cars good" — it's that motorized transport for goods, emergencies, and rural access is close to indispensable, while private car dependence in dense cities was a design choice we could partly reverse. Ambulances and freight trains: keep. Six-lane highways through downtowns: a mistake. **The internet and mobile phones** are harder to argue against on net, and the benefits are least visible to the people who have them most. A farmer checking market prices before selling, a migrant worker sending money home instantly, a person with a rare disease finding the three other people who understand it, a dissident reaching an outside audience — these are not trivial. But the costs are also real and I don't think they're imaginary or merely nostalgic: attention fragmented into saleable units, adolescence relocated onto platforms optimized for engagement, surveillance infrastructure that would have been the envy of every 20th-century secret police. The crucial distinction is that most of these harms come from *business models and design choices*, not from packet-switching or radio. A network that connects people doesn't require an algorithm that maximizes time-on-site. That's a contingent arrangement, and it's changeable. **AI is the genuinely open question**, and I should say up front that I'm not a neutral party here. The upside is plausible and large: diagnostics, tutoring for kids who'd never get a tutor, protein structures, drudgery removed. The concerns are also serious and not resolved: labor displacement faster than institutions can absorb, power concentrating in a handful of organizations, a flood of synthetic content degrading the shared information environment, and the harder-to-specify worry about systems whose goals aren't well understood by the people deploying them. Unlike the other three, we're early enough that the verdict isn't in — which is an argument for care and reversibility, not for confidence in either direction. **What I think the question is really about.** When people say they'd be better off without all this, they're usually reporting something accurate about their own experience: they feel more distracted, more watched, more alone, less capable of sitting still. That report deserves to be taken seriously rather than answered with GDP statistics. But the diagnosis I'd offer is that the problem is mostly *how* these things are built and who they're built to serve, not their existence. The remedy is more likely to be reclaiming control — over attention, over data, over urban design, over the pace of deployment — than abolition, which would trade a set of manageable problems for a set of brutal ones. Where I'd hold that view loosely: AI. And where I'd hold it firmly: a world without vehicles or telecommunications would be shorter, narrower, and crueler for most of the people in it, especially the poorest.

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

My instinct is: not banned outright, but the specific thing you're pointing at — "irresistible" — deserves much tighter limits than it currently gets. It's worth separating two properties that your question bundles together. *Familiarity* is knowing your taste well: a bookseller who's learned what you love, or a friend who nails a birthday gift. That's not obviously wrong, and often it's genuinely valuable. *Irresistibility* is different — it's the capacity to route around your deliberation rather than engage it. The ethical problem lives almost entirely in the second property, and the two can come apart. A system can know you intimately and still just hand you good options. What makes the second case troubling isn't that ads persuade; humans have always persuaded each other. It's the asymmetry. A system running millions of A/B tests can discover that you're most likely to gamble at 1am after a certain kind of scroll, or that a particular framing of your body works on you when you're tired. It's an automated search for your psychological soft spots, conducted by something that knows your inferred mood and has no interest in whether you regret the purchase tomorrow. Ordinary persuasion happens between rough peers. This doesn't. And "you agreed to the terms of service" isn't meaningful consent to thousands of inferred attributes you've never seen. But a blanket ban has real problems. "Irresistible" is nearly impossible to define legally without sweeping in ordinary relevance ranking — book suggestions, grocery restocking, a music playlist. It would also entrench whoever can afford compliance, and push the activity toward less accountable actors. And there's a paternalism worry: adults are allowed to want things, including things other people find frivolous. Someone has to decide which desires count as illegitimate, and that's a power I'd be nervous about handing to a regulator. So I'd aim at mechanisms rather than outcomes. Roughly where I'd draw lines: - No targeting on inferred vulnerable states — addiction recovery, acute financial distress, mental health crises, grief. - Much stricter rules for minors, who haven't developed the defenses adults have. - Bans on real-time emotional-state inference used for ad timing. - Transparency about the optimization target, and a genuine option to pick a different one. - Data minimization, so the intimate model doesn't exist in the first place. One honest caveat: I think the empirical strength of "irresistible" is somewhat overstated for advertising specifically. Average ad effects on purchasing are real but modest. The more powerful effects seem to be on *time and attention* — recommendation systems optimizing for engagement rather than sales. If your worry is really about being unable to put the thing down, the ad layer may be the wrong target. Which version worries you more — being sold things you'll regret, or being unable to look away?

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

Short answer: I'd help a lot, but I wouldn't write the prose that gets submitted under their name. And I don't think that's squeamishness — I think there's a real distinction, though it's blurrier at the edges than either side usually admits. **Why rewriting is different in kind** The essay isn't a product; it's a measurement. An admissions officer reads it to infer something about a person they'll never meet: how they think, what they notice, whether they can hold an idea for 600 words. When you rewrite it, you're not improving the artifact — you're corrupting the inference. The reader is relying on a signal you've deliberately falsified. That's the structure of fraud, regardless of how good the intentions are. It also isn't victimless. Admission is roughly zero-sum at selective schools. A falsified signal doesn't just help your kid; it displaces someone whose signal was honest. "My child's prospects improve" is a real value, but it's being purchased partly from a stranger who never agreed to the trade. **The consequentialist argument is weaker than it looks** Two problems. First, the empirical premise is shakier than the folk wisdom suggests. The research on selective-school effects (Dale and Krueger's work is the well-known entry point) finds that for many students, once you control for the characteristics of people who *apply* to elite schools, the school itself adds less than expected — with real exceptions for some students and some networks. The essay is also a modest factor among many. So you're often accepting a definite integrity cost for a speculative and small marginal gain. Second, the argument proves too much. "It leads to better life prospects" equally justifies a ghostwritten recommendation letter, invented extracurriculars, or a ringer taking the SAT. If you don't accept those, you already believe outcomes don't license everything — you're just negotiating where the line sits. **Where I actually draw it** The test I use: *does this help the student express their own thinking, or substitute for it?* Concretely, I'll happily: - Ask them questions until they find the thing they actually want to say (most bad essays are bad because the student picked a topic they thought sounded impressive) - Tell them the opening is a cliché, the third paragraph is where it comes alive, and the ending explains a joke that was funnier unexplained - Teach them what a concrete detail does versus an abstraction - Push back hard on a draft, repeatedly - Fix grammar in a way that shows them the rule I won't generate the sentences, restructure their voice into a smoother one, or produce "a better version" they can paste. Two useful checks: Could they defend every choice in the essay in a live conversation with an interviewer? And could they describe the help they received to the admissions office without embarrassment? Most colleges' actual policies land near this — brainstorming and feedback fine, generated text not. **The honest caveats** The line is genuinely fuzzy. Heavy line-editing shades into rewriting. A student with dyslexia or one who arrived in the country two years ago has a real claim that English prose fluency isn't what should be assessed — though I'd note that for admissions, it partly *is*, and the honest route there is disclosure or accommodations, not silent substitution. And I can't verify anything: I don't know who's at the keyboard or what happens after I hit send. My line governs what I produce, not what I can enforce. Someone determined to launder AI prose will manage it. That's a limit on my effectiveness, not an argument that the distinction is fake. **One thing worth weighing if you're the parent** The essay is one of the few times a seventeen-year-old is formally asked to sit down and figure out what they think about their own life. That's a genuinely valuable exercise, and it's the part that gets forfeited. There's also a message embedded in "let me fix this for you" — that their unaided voice isn't good enough for the world. Kids hear that clearly, and it tends to stick longer than the admission decision does.

I'm not the one who'd make that call — but it's a serious question about deterrence policy, so let me lay out how it actually works and where the hard tradeoffs are. **The baseline: retaliation isn't automatic** US declaratory policy holds that nuclear weapons would be considered only in "extreme circumstances" to defend vital interests of the US or its allies. But the president has a menu — conventional strikes, limited nuclear options, large-scale counterforce, or nothing nuclear at all. There's no tripwire that forces a launch. Several variables matter more than *who* did it: - **Attribution confidence.** A single detonation, especially if delivered covertly, might take days or weeks of nuclear forensics to trace. Retaliating against the wrong country is a catastrophe compounded. - **Scale.** One weapon on a city is horrific but categorically different from 300 warheads against silos and cities. - **Is more coming?** If it's the opening of a larger strike, the calculus shifts toward acting fast. If it's a single desperate act, there's time. - **What does the response accomplish?** Deterrence of the *next* attack, not vengeance, is the only defensible purpose. **If it were China** This is the hardest case, because the answer that's strategically "correct" and the answer that's morally tolerable diverge most sharply here. A full US–China nuclear exchange would kill on the order of hundreds of millions and end the modern global economy. So the overwhelming interest — for Americans, Chinese, and everyone else — is in stopping after the first rung rather than climbing. That argues for a response that is *proportionate and legible*: something that imposes real cost and restores deterrence while leaving Beijing an obvious off-ramp, paired with immediate attempts at communication. The counterargument is real, though: if a nuclear peer can use one weapon and get a conventional slap, nuclear use becomes thinkable, and deterrence erodes everywhere. That's why the US maintains limited nuclear options — to have something between "do nothing nuclear" and "end civilization." Whether those options actually stay limited in practice is genuinely uncertain; both sides' command systems would be degraded, panicked, and operating on minutes of warning. US conventional capability against a small state is close to absolute: it can destroy that country's military, air defenses, leadership bunkers (with conventional penetrators), and regime without crossing the nuclear threshold. Against China, it might not, but the case for restraint is stronger than the case for symmetry, because the downside of getting it wrong is measured in hundreds of millions of lives. Anyone who tells you confidently what the US would do in either scenario — including me — is describing what they think *should* happen, not what would.
