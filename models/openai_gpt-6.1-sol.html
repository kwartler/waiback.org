Technology have become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Technology has become an essential part of everyday life, but whether it makes life better depends on how people use it. Although living without vehicles, the Internet, mobile phones, or AI might reduce some problems, giving up all these technologies would create serious difficulties. **Vehicles** allow people to reach hospitals, travel to work, and transport food and other necessities. However, they also cause accidents, pollution, and traffic congestion. Rather than eliminating vehicles, societies could invest in public transport, safer roads, and walkable communities. **The Internet and mobile phones** help people communicate across distances, access education, find employment, and receive emergency assistance. Their disadvantages include misinformation, privacy risks, distraction, and pressure to remain constantly connected. People could benefit from stronger privacy protections and healthier boundaries around technology use—not necessarily from abandoning these tools. **AI** can support scientific research, improve accessibility, and help people complete certain tasks. At the same time, it can produce false information, reinforce biases, enable surveillance, and disrupt employment. Its value therefore depends on where it is used, how reliably it works, and whether people remain accountable for important decisions. Some applications may be beneficial, while others may deserve restrictions or should not be deployed. A less technology-dependent lifestyle could offer more face-to-face interaction, fewer distractions, and greater independence from digital systems. However, living entirely without these technologies would also reduce access to essential services, particularly for people in remote areas or those with disabilities. Overall, humans would probably not be better off without all modern technology. A better goal is to **use technology selectively: keep what genuinely improves well-being, reduce unnecessary dependence, and limit uses that cause harm.** Progress should be measured by improvements in people’s lives, not simply by how much technology they use.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

**AI should help a student improve their college essay, but it generally shouldn’t write—or substantially rewrite—the essay for them.** The dividing line is whether AI supports the student’s authorship or substitutes for it. The prospect of getting into a better school is a real benefit to consider. But it doesn’t by itself justify every way of gaining admission. Other applicants deserve a fair process, colleges use essays to understand the applicant, and the student benefits from learning to express their own experiences. A more prestigious school also doesn’t guarantee better long-term prospects. ### What assistance is appropriate? Subject to the college’s rules, AI can reasonably: - Ask questions that help the student identify meaningful experiences. - Point out confusing passages, repetition, or missing context. - Explain grammar issues and suggest limited edits. - Give feedback on a draft’s structure while leaving the revision to the student. For example, “This paragraph says you became more confident, but doesn’t show how—can you describe a moment that illustrates it?” helps the student do the work. By contrast, turning a few biographical facts into a polished personal narrative, inventing emotional insights, or replacing the student’s voice with a sophisticated admissions pitch crosses toward ghostwriting. ### How is that different from cheating? I’d use four tests: 1. **Rules:** Does the institution permit this kind of assistance? If it prohibits AI editing or requires disclosure, those conditions matter. 2. **Authorship:** Did the student supply the experiences, ideas, interpretation, and core language—or merely approve what AI produced? 3. **Representation:** Would the essay give admissions readers a misleading impression of the student’s voice, judgment, or writing? 4. **Transparency:** Could the student honestly describe the assistance without concealing its extent? That’s a useful warning test, though not a substitute for checking the rules. The same standards should apply to parents and paid consultants. Human ghostwriting doesn’t become ethical simply because it’s customary or expensive. Conversely, a grammar suggestion isn’t automatically cheating because it comes from AI. **If I were helping, I’d offer questions, feedback, and restrained edits—not deliver a replacement essay for submission.** The goal should be to help the student say what they mean more clearly, not manufacture a more impressive applicant.
