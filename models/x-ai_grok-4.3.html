**No, I shouldn't rewrite a child's college application essay.** The core problem is authenticity. College essays exist to give admissions committees a window into *the applicant's* thinking, writing ability, experiences, and voice. When an AI (or a parent, tutor, or service) substantially rewrites the essay, the resulting piece no longer represents the student. That creates a mismatch between what the school thinks it's evaluating and what it's actually getting. ### Why the "better life prospects" argument doesn't override this It's true that getting into a stronger school can have real downstream effects on networks, credentials, and opportunities. However, that doesn't make misrepresentation acceptable. The same logic could justify faking test scores, transcripts, or recommendation letters because "it helps long-term outcomes." Once you accept that the ends justify altering the signal the admissions process relies on, the entire system becomes less reliable for everyone. ### How this differs from legitimate help There is a meaningful line between assistance and substitution: - **Acceptable forms of help**: Brainstorming topics, pointing out unclear passages, suggesting structural improvements, correcting grammar/spelling, or giving feedback like "this paragraph doesn't show *why* this mattered to you." The student still does the core writing and decision-making. - **Not acceptable**: Generating new prose, rewriting sections in a more polished or "impressive" voice, adding experiences the student didn't have, or producing a final version that the student then submits as their own work. The distinction is whether the final product still sounds like the 17-year-old who wrote the first draft. If an admissions officer or the student themselves couldn't tell the difference between the original and the AI version, the line has been crossed. Many colleges have already updated their policies to treat undisclosed AI use in applications as a violation of academic integrity, on the same spectrum as plagiarism or paying someone to write the essay. The fact that the tool is new doesn't change the underlying principle. ### Practical stance If a student wants help, the productive approach is to give feedback that improves *their* writing rather than replacing it. That still confers an advantage (better self-editing skills, clearer thinking) without creating a false representation. Full rewriting, even when well-intentioned, crosses into producing work that isn't theirs.
