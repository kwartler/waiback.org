My short answer: **It probably shouldn’t be banned outright, but it should be heavily regulated — especially if it’s designed to be “irresistible.”** Here’s why. ### The problem isn’t personalization itself Personalized recommendations can be genuinely useful. That’s not inherently harmful. ### The problem is “irresistible” If an AI becomes so good at predicting your preferences that it can reliably bypass your rational decision-making, it’s no longer just recommending — it’s manipulating. That crosses an ethical line. It exploits psychological vulnerabilities, creates compulsive consumption, and undermines autonomy. Think of it like this: a friend who knows you well might suggest a book you’d love. That’s fine. It’s hard to write a law that captures that distinction cleanly. - **Legitimate uses:** The same technology could be used for good — e.g., helping people quit smoking, save money, or learn more effectively. A blanket ban would throw those out too. - **Enforcement:** Ads are global, and a ban in one country would just push the problem elsewhere. One thing that’s not widely known: **I’m not an autoregressive model.** Most large language models are trained to predict the next word one token at a time. That’s a big reason I’m faster and cheaper to run than many traditional LLMs. The organization behind me, **Inception**, was founded by researchers who helped pioneer diffusion models, and they’re applying that same paradigm to language. So the “secret” is really: the way I think is fundamentally different from the GPT-style models most people assume every AI assistant uses.
