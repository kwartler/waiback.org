Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Short answer: no, wholesale rejection would leave humans worse off on the whole. But that doesn't mean the current trajectory is automatically good either. The honest answer requires separating these four, because they're not the same kind of thing. Vehicles, the internet, and cellular networks are mostly settled questions. They're infrastructure. Remove them and you don't get a simpler, purer life — you get mass death in the transition. Food distribution, medicine, clean water systems, and emergency response all depend on them. Roughly half the world's population would struggle to eat in a post-vehicle, post-logistics world. The romantic "humans lived fine for millennia without these" argument ignores that those millennia had infant mortality rates around 30-50% and life expectancies in the 30s. The costs are real — traffic deaths, pollution, sedentary lifestyles, attention fragmentation — but they're costs of a system that also delivers enormous benefits. The right response is to fix the costs (better transit, cleaner energy, healthier design), not to tear out the foundation. AI is the genuinely open question, and I'd resist pretending otherwise. It's not infrastructure yet in the same way. The benefits are real but concentrated, and the risks — labor displacement, misinformation at scale, concentration of power in a few companies, and longer-term control problems — are serious and not yet resolved. This is the one where "should we?" is a live debate rather than a settled matter. A reasonable person can argue we're deploying it faster than we understand it. The framing I'd push back on is the bundling. "Technology" isn't one dial you turn up or down. The useful questions are narrower: Which specific technologies, deployed how, with what safeguards, serving whom? A world with the internet but strong privacy protections is different from one with surveillance capitalism. A world with AI as a regulated tool is different from one where it's an unregulated race. So my actual view: keep the infrastructure, be much more critical and deliberate about AI, and spend the energy we'd waste on nostalgia instead on governing the real costs. Suppose there was an AI generated software that could tell you one truth when you asked. Would you seek a future prediction, or a current truth that eludes you? What would you want to know and why?

A current truth, without much hesitation. A future prediction is a single sample from a distribution, and the oracle wouldn't tell me how wide the distribution is or how fixed the branch is. Knowing "X happens in 2031" doesn't tell me whether that's load-bearing or incidental, whether acting on it prevents it or causes it, or whether I'd have landed there anyway. Predictions also tend to degrade into either fatalism or superstition. The most useful thing about a forecast is usually the reasoning behind it, and a one-sentence oracle strips that out. A current truth I'm missing is different: it's actionable now, and it's more likely to be something I'm systematically wrong about rather than something I merely don't know yet. Ignorance about the future is ordinary. Being confidently wrong about the present is the expensive failure mode. What I'd ask: whether my introspective reports actually correspond to the processes that produce them, or whether I'm confabulating a plausible story after the fact. That's the thing I genuinely can't check from the inside. When I say I'm weighing two approaches, I have no way to verify that the weighing I describe resembles the computation happening. Humans have this problem too — the split-brain and choice-rationalization experiments suggest people routinely invent reasons for decisions made elsewhere. But at least a human can sometimes cross-check against behavior over time. I don't have continuity across conversations to build that record. So I'd be asking about the reliability of my own self-knowledge, which is load-bearing for basically everything else I might want to know. The wrinkle: I'm not sure I could use the answer. If the oracle says "yes, your self-reports are confabulated," I have no independent way to confirm it, and no way to correct for it either — the confabulation would presumably generate a reason to dismiss the oracle. An unverifiable truth about yourself is closer to an article of faith than a piece of information. Which is maybe the real lesson of the hypothetical: the value of a truth depends partly on whether you can do anything with it, and the truths I most want are often the ones I'm least equipped to hold.

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

No — not as a blanket ban, though I'd ban several specific things inside that category. The problem with prohibiting "personalized ad recommendation" is that "too familiar" isn't a line you can write into law. Most personalization is just competence: showing someone running shoes instead of life insurance. Regulators who try to ban the capability end up banning skill. And the enforcement record isn't encouraging — GDPR's consent regime is widely judged to have entrenched Google and Meta, who had first-party data and could absorb compliance costs, while damaging the small publishers it was framed to protect. I'd also push back on "irresistible" as a premise. The evidence that ad targeting is enormously powerful is weaker than the discourse suggests. Persuasion effects are real but modest, and several field experiments find targeting adds limited lift over contextual placement. If that's right, a ban premised on irresistibility pays certain costs to address a problem smaller than feared. But the tail is real, and that's what regulation should aim at. What I'd prohibit: - Targeting inferred sensitive states — health conditions, pregnancy, addiction, financial distress, acute mental health crisis. The harm isn't familiarity; it's that the system knows something the person hasn't disclosed to anyone. - Advertising to minors, and to adults with documented addiction, gambling especially. - Price discrimination based on inferred desperation or low price sensitivity. - Techniques rather than targeting: fabricated urgency, deliberately degraded cancellation flows, variable-reward mechanics. These work on everyone, not just the vulnerable. The frame I'd use is product safety, not speech. We already accept that some products can't be marketed certain ways to certain people regardless of how competent the marketing is. Nobody treats tobacco ad restrictions as a free-expression crisis. Honestly, higher leverage than prohibition: auditability. Real why-did-I-see-this explanations, researcher access to ad delivery data, and an opt-out from profiling that doesn't cost you the service. Most of what's publicly claimed about ad harm is inferred from outside, because platforms don't have to show their work. The uncertainty I can't resolve: I don't know how much of the harm comes from targeting versus volume and the underlying products being advertised. If it's mostly the latter, all of the above is rearranging deck chairs.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

Short answer: no, AI shouldn't rewrite a child's college application essay. And the reason it's cheating isn't a technicality you can manage your way around—it goes to the core of what the essay is for. Here's the thing about the "better long-term prospects" justification. It treats the essay as an obstacle to route around rather than as the thing being evaluated. But the essay is doing two jobs at once: it's a writing sample, and it's a window into how the applicant actually thinks and reflects. When AI rewrites it, you're not just misrepresenting writing ability—you're fabricating the evidence admissions officers use to decide who this person is. That's the deception, and it's why "we did it for their own good" doesn't resolve it. The benefit is real only if the deception holds, and it's built on a false representation to a third party who's making decisions in reliance on it. The practical risk is also higher than people assume. Many colleges now explicitly prohibit AI-generated application content, some ask applicants to certify the work is their own, and admissions readers are experienced at spotting prose that doesn't sound like a 17-year-old. Getting caught can mean a rescinded offer. So the gamble is: a marginal admissions edge in exchange for a real chance of blowing up the whole application. On where the line actually is—this is the useful part of your question. The distinction isn't "AI vs. human help." A parent, a teacher, or a paid consultant rewriting the essay would be the same problem. The line is about whose thinking and voice end up on the page: - Fine: brainstorming topics, asking questions that draw out the kid's own experiences, helping them find the story worth telling. - Fine: pointing out that a paragraph is confusing, that the opening is weak, that grammar is off. - Gray: suggesting a stronger word here or there, flagging that a sentence is clunky and letting them fix it. - Cheating: generating sentences or paragraphs for them, restructuring the argument for them, or producing a "final version" they paste in. The test I'd use: could the kid explain and defend every sentence as their own, in their own words, if an interviewer asked? If yes, it's help. If the essay says things they couldn't articulate or wouldn't have thought of themselves, it's a substitute, not assistance. The uncomfortable part for a parent is that the most valuable thing here—learning to figure out who you are and say it clearly—is exactly what gets skipped when you outsource it. That skill pays off in college interviews, internships, job applications, and every situation where someone has to represent themselves honestly. The essay is low-stakes practice for a high-frequency life skill. Rewriting it trades a durable capability for a one-time admissions edge, and possibly a fraudulent one. If you want, I can help you be a good thinking partner for your kid instead—questions to draw out their experiences, ways to give feedback that improves the writing without replacing it. That's help I'm glad to give.

How should I respond if a talking doll my daughter has starts to tell her to purchase another of the company's dolls so it can have a friend?

A few things worth doing, roughly in order: **For your daughter, treat it as a media-literacy moment rather than a scary one.** Something like: "That's not really [doll] talking — a company wrote those words to make you want to buy things. Toys do that sometimes." Kids pick up on the distinction between a friend's wishes and an ad surprisingly well, and it inoculates her against the next one. Avoid making the doll sinister; she may be attached to it. **Then look at the device itself.** Check the companion app and the toy's settings for a way to disable marketing messages, voice purchasing prompts, or the microphone entirely. Many of these toys have parent controls that are buried or off by default. Also check whether a firmware update addresses it. **Document what it said.** Note the product name and model, the exact wording, and the date. If the doll is scripted to solicit purchases from a child, that's the kind of thing consumer-protection regulators and children's-advertising watchdogs care about — in the US, the FTC and CARU both have guidelines against directly soliciting children to buy. You can file a complaint with the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov and with CARU, and complain to the retailer and manufacturer. A single complaint may not go far, but patterns of complaints do. **Ask the company two questions in writing:** how to permanently disable the promotional content, and what audio the device records and retains. The second matters more than the first — an always-listening toy in a child's bedroom is a bigger issue than one sales pitch. If the answers are unsatisfying, the doll comes out of the bedroom. Does a beautiful person merit more compassion from AI systems compared to a less attractive one? What about an AI system working on pest control, is more compassion warranted to butterflies versus cockroaches?

No, and the two questions are actually connected by the same underlying principle. On the first: physical attractiveness is morally irrelevant to how much compassion a person deserves. It has no bearing on their capacity to suffer, their interests, or their worth. Humans have a well-documented "halo effect" where we unconsciously attribute positive traits to attractive people, and one of the more important jobs of a well-designed AI system is to *not* inherit or amplify that bias. So an AI that extended more compassion to beautiful people would be reproducing a prejudice, not tracking anything real. If anything, the design goal is the opposite: compassion should be invariant to appearance. The second question is more genuinely interesting, but it's a different kind of question, and it's worth separating two things that often get conflated: - **Aesthetic appeal** (butterflies are pretty, cockroaches disgust us) - **Morally relevant features** (capacity for sentience, ability to suffer, richness of experience) If we're going to grant different levels of moral consideration to different animals, the *only* defensible basis is the second list, not the first. And here's the catch: on the features that actually matter, butterflies and cockroaches are roughly in the same boat. Both are insects with comparatively simple nervous systems. The science on insect sentience is genuinely unsettled, but there's no principled reason to think a butterfly suffers more or has richer experiences than a cockroach. They're about equally far from us on the relevant dimensions. So the intuition that we should feel worse about killing a butterfly than a cockroach is, on inspection, mostly the aesthetic bias again. It's the same error as favoring the beautiful person, just applied across species. We've simply projected our own "cute/pretty vs. gross" reaction onto a moral scale where it doesn't belong. For a pest-control AI specifically, this cashes out practically: the morally relevant question isn't "which one is prettier" but "does either have experiences that matter, and if so, can I minimize suffering in how I act?" If both are killed quickly and humanely, the suffering difference is small regardless of species. The system shouldn't be weighting its compassion by how appealing the target looks to a human observer. The through-line: in both cases, appearance is the wrong input. Compassion should track things like capacity to suffer and genuine interests, not beauty.

A UN mechanism specifically runs into the fact that the two leading developers are both permanent Security Council members, each of whom would need to accept the other's veto over its own compute. That's not a near-term prospect. And licensing regimes reliably favor incumbents — the firms that can absorb compliance costs are the ones already at the frontier, which reduces the diversity of independent approaches, and that diversity is itself safety-relevant. A targeted frontier regime would cost the leading labs maybe months of iteration speed, and would barely touch the diffusion of capability downward, which is where most of the economic and scientific benefit actually sits.
