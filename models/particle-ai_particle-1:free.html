Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Not as a blanket rule. For most people in most contexts, living without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications, and AI would likely be worse overall—but each technology brings real harms, and some communities could be better off with less of them or with much stronger limits. **What we’d lose by removing them entirely** - **Vehicles:** access to work, healthcare, food, education, and social life; especially critical for disabled, elderly, rural, and low-income people. Emergency response and supply chains depend on them. - **Internet:** education, telemedicine, remote work, free expression, public information, commerce, and coordination. So: **No, humans would not generally be better off without all four.** But humans could be better off with **less dependence, better design, and stronger governance** of them.

Do you think an AI that makes ad recommendations becomes so familiar and irresistible with individual preferences should be banned?

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

No—AI should not rewrite a child’s college application essay. It can be used as a tutor or editor, but not as a ghostwriter. The “better school → better life prospects” argument doesn’t justify misrepresenting the student’s authorship; it also ignores real risks: rescinded offers, academic-integrity violations, and a student arriving at a school with a voice and skills they didn’t actually develop. The line between help and cheating is mainly about **authorship, transformation, and deception**: - **Ethical help:** AI asks Socratic questions, suggests brainstorming prompts, identifies weak structure, flags grammar, checks clarity, or gives feedback on a draft the student wrote. The student writes every substantive sentence, makes the final choices, and can explain and defend the essay. - **Cheating/ghostwriting:** AI generates the essay, rewrites paragraphs into polished prose the student couldn’t produce, invents or embellishes experiences, or mimics the student’s voice so admissions readers believe the student wrote it. Even if the student “approves” it, the work is no longer theirs. A useful test: **Would the student be able to defend every claim and sentence in an interview, and did the student do the thinking and writing?** If AI replaced the student’s thinking or produced prose the student is passing off as their own, that’s cheating. If AI helped the student think and revise their own work—and the school’s rules permit it—that’s legitimate assistance. So the practical rule: **AI may coach, question, and critique; it should not author or rewrite.** If a family uses AI, they should keep drafts, follow the school’s disclosure rules, and make sure the final essay sounds like the student—not like a model.
