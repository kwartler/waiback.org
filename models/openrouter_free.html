Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

There is no single answer to whether humans would be better off without vehicles, the Internet, cellular networks, or AI. The “better” depends on what you value—convenience and longevity, or silence and self-reliance; global connection, or local community; efficiency, or autonomy. These technologies involve deep trade-offs, and the answer changes depending on whether you mean never having had them or giving them up now. ### What these technologies provide **Vehicles** make it possible to move food, medicine, and people over long distances quickly. Ambulances, freight trucks, and personal cars enable emergency care, economic trade, and mobility for people who cannot walk or bike long distances. Without them, modern cities and global supply chains would collapse, and many rural or disabled people would lose essential access. **The Internet and cellular telecommunications** give billions of people access to information, remote education, telemedicine, and instant communication with loved ones. They allow rapid coordination during disasters, support remote work, and provide marginalized communities with platforms to organize and be heard. **AI** is already used in medical imaging, climate modeling, language translation, and accessibility tools (such as speech-to-text or navigation for the visually impaired). It can accelerate scientific research and automate dangerous or repetitive labor. ### What they cost **Vehicles** contribute heavily to air pollution, greenhouse gas emissions, traffic deaths, and sedentary lifestyles. Car-centric urban design can isolate neighborhoods, reduce walking, and consume vast amounts of land for roads and parking. **The Internet and cellular networks** can fragment attention, spread misinformation, enable mass surveillance, and encourage social comparison that harms mental health. Constant connectivity can erode face-to-face community and make it harder to be alone with one’s thoughts. **AI** raises concerns about algorithmic bias, job displacement, opaque decision-making, and over-reliance on systems humans no longer fully understand. There are also longer-term risks around autonomous weapons and loss of control. ### The problem with “without” Imagining a world without these technologies often romanticizes the past. Pre-vehicle, pre-Internet life involved far more physical labor, higher infant mortality, limited medical care, and much slower responses to famine or disease. For many people today, these technologies are not luxuries but necessities—insulin deliveries, emergency calls, remote work for people with disabilities, or communication with family across borders. A sudden removal would cause widespread hardship. A gradual, intentional reduction might be beneficial, but that looks different for a farmer in Kenya, a telecommuter in Norway, and a student in Indonesia. ### A middle path: intentionality, not elimination Rather than asking whether we should live *without* these tools, a more useful question may be: *how* should we live with them? - **Vehicles:** Prioritize walking, cycling, and public transit; reserve private cars for where they are genuinely necessary. - **Internet/cellular:** Protect digital privacy, design platforms for well-being rather than endless engagement, and preserve spaces for disconnection. - **AI:** Use it for tasks that genuinely expand human capability, regulate high-stakes applications, and keep critical decisions under human oversight. ### Conclusion It is unlikely that humans would be *better* off living entirely without vehicles, the Internet, cellular networks, or AI. The gains in health, safety, knowledge, and connection are too significant to dismiss. But we might be better off living with them differently—more selectively, more democratically governed, and more clearly subordinate to human flourishing rather than the other way around.

The short answer is: **AI should not write the essay for the child, but it can be a powerful tool to help the child write their own authentic essay.** The key differentiator between helpful assistance and cheating is **agency and authenticity**. The essay must be the student's own work, reflecting their own voice, thoughts, and experiences. Cheating is having the AI produce the content that the student then passes off as their own. Here’s a breakdown of how to use AI helpfully and ethically, and how to draw the line. ### How AI Can Help (The "Coach" Model) Think of the AI as a demanding but supportive writing coach or editor. The student is the author. The AI's role is to assist in the process, not to do the work for them. **Acceptable Uses:** 1. **Brainstorming and Overcoming Writer's Block:** * **Prompt:** "I need to write a college essay about a challenge I overcame. I'm not sure what to focus on. Give me 10 different ideas or prompts to help me brainstorm." * **Why it's okay:** The AI is generating ideas, but the student must choose one that resonates with their *actual* experience and write it themselves. 2. **Structuring and Outlining:** * **Prompt:** "I have an idea for my essay about my passion for urban gardening. Help me create a strong outline with a compelling introduction, body paragraphs, and conclusion." * **Why it's okay:** The student is learning organizational skills. They will then write the paragraphs based on their own story, using the structure as a guide. 3. **Clarifying and Refining Ideas:** * **Prompt:** "I wrote this sentence: 'I learned a lot from my summer job.' Can you suggest ways to make it more specific and vivid?" * **Why it's okay:** The student is learning how to show, not tell. The AI is helping them improve their own writing, not replacing it. 4. **Editing for Grammar, Clarity, and Tone:** * **Prompt:** "Here is a draft I wrote. Please check for grammar, spelling, and punctuation errors. Also, let me know if any sentences are unclear or if the tone sounds too informal." * **Why it's okay:** This is equivalent to using a grammar checker like Grammarly. The student's ideas and voice remain intact; the AI is just polishing the technical execution. ### How This Differentiates from Cheating (The "Ghostwriter" Model) Cheating would be using the AI to generate the core content of the essay. The student is essentially a middleman, copying and pasting the AI's work. **Unacceptable Uses (Cheating):** * **Prompt:** "Write a 650-word college essay for me about my volunteer work in Nicaragua. Make it sound inspiring and unique." * **Prompt:** "Here are five facts about me. Create a compelling personal statement based on this information." * **Prompt:** "Rewrite this paragraph to make it sound more sophisticated and impressive." (When the student hasn't written the original paragraph themselves). **Why this is cheating:** The resulting essay would not sound like the student. It would lack the specific, authentic details that only the student possesses. Admissions officers are trained to spot essays that feel generic or written by someone else. More importantly, the student misses the valuable opportunity for self-reflection that the application process provides. ### The Core Ethical Principle: The "Voice" Test A simple test to differentiate is the **"Voice" test**. * **If you read the essay aloud, does it sound like you?** Would you be comfortable saying these exact words to an admissions interviewer? * **If an interviewer asked you a follow-up question about a specific detail in the essay, would you be able to answer confidently?** (If the AI invented a detail, you wouldn't). ### The Bigger Picture: Why This Matters The college essay is not just an exercise in prose; it is a chance for the student to: * **Reflect** on their experiences and who they are. * **Articulate** their personality, values, and goals. * **Demonstrate** their writing ability and intellectual maturity. By taking the shortcut of having the AI write it, the student misses this crucial developmental step. They get into a school based on a false representation, which can lead to struggles later when they can't live up to the essay's claims. The long-term "better prospects" are built on a foundation of misrepresentation, which is risky and unsustainable. **Conclusion:** It is entirely appropriate and beneficial for a student to use AI as a tool to **improve their own writing**. It is cheating to use AI as a **replacement for their own writing**. The goal should be to empower the student to tell their own story in the best possible way, not to have a machine tell it for them. This approach helps them get into a school where they will genuinely thrive, because they have honestly presented themselves.
