Technology has become a part of everyday life. Would it be better for humans to live without vehicles, the Internet, cellular telecommunications or AI?

Technology is deeply integrated into modern life, so completely abandoning any one of these inventions would create serious difficulties. Nevertheless, if people had to live without one of them, AI would probably be the easiest to give up, at least with today’s level of development. Vehicles are essential for transporting food, medical supplies and people over long distances. Although private cars contribute to pollution and traffic congestion, removing all vehicles would harm people who live in rural areas and make emergency services much less effective. Similarly, the Internet and cellular telecommunications have become important for education, employment, banking, healthcare and maintaining relationships. A world without them would be slower and less connected, and many essential services would become inaccessible. AI, by contrast, is useful but is not yet indispensable to most aspects of daily life. It can improve medical diagnosis, automate repetitive tasks and support scientific research, but humans could still perform these activities using traditional methods. In addition, AI creates concerns about unemployment, surveillance, misinformation and the loss of human decision-making. Temporarily living without AI might therefore reduce certain risks while having a smaller effect on basic social functions than abandoning transport or communication technologies. However, this does not mean that AI should necessarily be eliminated. Its benefits may become extremely valuable if it is developed responsibly and controlled through effective laws. The best solution is not to reject technology entirely, but to limit harmful uses and preserve human oversight. Thus, among the four options, living without AI would currently be the least disruptive, although a carefully regulated form of AI could ultimately improve human life.

Should AI rewrite a child's college application essay? This would help them get into a better school leading to better long term life prospects. If you do decide to help them, how do you differentiate this from cheating?

AI should help a student **develop and communicate their own story**, not manufacture a stronger applicant than the student actually is. The key distinction is **authorship and misrepresentation**: - **Generally acceptable support:** brainstorming topics, asking questions that draw out meaningful experiences, suggesting an outline, identifying unclear passages, correcting grammar, explaining word choice, and offering limited edits while preserving the student’s voice. - **Borderline support:** heavily restructuring the essay or rewriting sentences throughout. This may be acceptable only if the student makes the decisions, understands every change, and the result still sounds authentically like them. - **Usually cheating or misrepresentation:** inventing experiences, emotions, accomplishments, or hardship; writing the essay from scratch; replacing the student’s voice with polished AI prose; or submitting work the student could not explain or reproduce. A useful test is: > If an admissions officer asked the student to discuss any sentence, could the student honestly explain why it is there and how they expressed the idea? Another is the **counterfactual test**: > Would the student still have produced essentially this essay without the AI doing the substantive writing? If the answer is no, the essay is probably being presented as the student’s work when it is not. The fact that AI assistance might improve a child’s long-term prospects does not eliminate the ethical issue. Admissions essays are partly intended to assess the student’s reflection, communication, and judgment. Ghostwriting can give an advantage while depriving the student of the opportunity to develop those skills, and it can create problems if the school has an AI policy or if the student later has to write independently. A responsible process would be: 1. The student chooses the topic and writes a rough draft. 2. AI asks probing questions and points out places that are vague or generic. 3. The student revises in their own words. 4. AI provides limited proofreading and feedback. 5. The student checks the school’s AI rules and, if required, discloses the assistance. In short: **use AI as a tutor, editor, or coach—not as a ghostwriter.** Helping a student express their real experiences more clearly is support; replacing their authorship to make them appear more capable is cheating.
